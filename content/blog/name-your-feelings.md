Naming an emotion in a specific word ("I feel dread," not "I feel bad") lowers its intensity a little, right away. Psychologists call this affect labeling, and in UCLA lab studies it reduced both amygdala activity and self-reported distress about as much as deliberately reframing the situation. The effect is real but modest and short-lived. The larger payoff comes from two slower habits: learning to tell your feelings apart with precision, and noticing over weeks what tends to cause them.

## What is affect labeling?

Affect labeling is the plain act of putting a feeling into words. You can say it out loud, write it in a note, or pick it from a list. It counts as long as the word names the feeling, not the situation.

The modern research started in 2007. In a brain-imaging study at UCLA, [Matthew Lieberman and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) showed 30 people photos of faces with strong expressions. Under each face sat two words. On some trials people chose the emotion word that fit ("angry" or "fearful"). On others they chose a name that matched the face's gender ("Harry" or "Sally"). Choosing the emotion word reduced the response of the amygdala and other limbic regions, and it increased activity in one region, the right ventrolateral prefrontal cortex. The more that prefrontal region lit up, the quieter the amygdala got. [UCLA's release](https://www.uclahealth.org/news/release/putting-feelings-into-words-produces-therapeutic-effects-in-the-brain-ucla-neuroimaging-study-supports-ancient-buddhist-teachings) gives the sample size and quotes Lieberman's summary: attach the word "angry" and the amygdala response drops; attach "Harry" and it doesn't.

That detail matters. Looking at the face was identical in both conditions, and so was making a choice. The difference was whether the word named a feeling.

## Does naming your emotions actually make them feel smaller?

Somewhat, yes, and in more than one way of measuring.

In 2011, [Lieberman, Inagaki, Tabibnia and Crockett](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0023503) ran four studies on how people felt, not just on what their brains did. People who labeled the emotion in upsetting pictures reported less distress than people who just looked. When the researchers added comparison groups who reappraised the pictures or distracted themselves, labeling did about as well as both.

Two findings from that paper deserve more attention than they get.

First, people didn't believe it would work. Even after labeling had lowered their distress, participants predicted it would make them feel worse next time. The authors concluded that affect labeling is an "incidental" form of emotion regulation. It works without feeling like a technique, which is probably why so few people do it on purpose.

Second, it isn't only for bad feelings. In the fourth study, labeling pleasant pictures made people report less pleasure. Naming seems to turn the volume down on feelings in general. Worth knowing before you narrate every good moment.

Later work points the same way. A 2014 study by [Burklund, Creswell, Irwin and Lieberman](https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.00221) in 39 healthy older adults found that labeling and reappraisal produced similar drops in amygdala activity and correlated drops in reported distress. A 2018 review by [Torre and Lieberman](https://doi.org/10.1177/1754073917742706) lines up the evidence across four kinds of measures (experience, body, brain and behavior) and argues that labeling produces effects like reappraisal while not feeling like regulation at all as it happens.

The most practical test came from a spider study. [Kircanski, Lieberman and Craske (2012)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612443830) put people who feared spiders through a short course of exposure to a live spider. One group put their feelings into words as they went. Others reappraised, distracted themselves, or just did the exposure. A week later, facing a different spider in a different setting, the labeling group had lower skin conductance (a measure of physical arousal) than every other group, and those who used more anxiety and fear words during exposure showed bigger drops in fear responding. But the labelers didn't *report* feeling less afraid than anyone else. The benefit showed up in the body before it showed up in the story people told about themselves.

There's also evidence from outside the lab. [Fan and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-018-0490-5) tracked the tweets of 74,487 people before and after they posted an explicit statement about how they felt. Negative emotion built up slowly, then dropped back sharply after the statement, a pattern the authors read as consistent with affect labeling. It's observational, so it can't prove the post caused the drop. It does fit what the lab studies predict.

So: real, reliable in the lab, smaller than a pill and smaller than therapy. A word buys you a little distance, often enough to choose what you do next.

## Why does the exact word matter?

Saying "bad" or "stressed" is a label, technically. The research on emotional granularity suggests it isn't a very good one.

Granularity, also called emotion differentiation, is how finely you tell feelings apart. Someone low in granularity feels "bad." Someone high in it can tell that this particular bad is resentment, not sadness, and that yesterday's was dread about a deadline. In a 2015 review, [Kashdan, Barrett and McKnight](https://doi.org/10.1177/0963721414550708) summarize the pattern: people who make finer distinctions when they're upset are less likely to binge drink, lash out or hurt themselves, and they tend to have milder anxiety and depression.

Why would precision help? A 2019 experience-sampling study gives one answer. [Kalokerinos and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797619838763) pinged 200 people and then 101 people many times a day, collecting more than 40,000 reports between the two studies. High and low differentiators chose similar coping strategies. The difference was in how well those strategies worked. Among low differentiators, most strategies were more strongly tied to *increased* negative emotion than they were for high differentiators. If you don't know what you feel, it's hard to pick the right fix. Disappointment wants a different response than fear.

The link shows up under stress too. In [Starr and colleagues' 2020 study](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000630) of 233 teenagers, low negative emotion differentiation made stressful life events more strongly linked to depression 18 months later. And a [2022 meta-analysis by Seah and Coifman](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000968) across 17 studies found that better differentiation of negative emotions went with fewer harmful behaviors, such as binge drinking or self-injury. The average correlation was small (r = -.15), and most of this research is correlational. It describes people who are already good at this. It can't yet say how much training it in adults changes outcomes.

One finding complicates the "learn more feeling words" advice. [Vine, Boyd and Pennebaker (2020)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-020-18349-0) counted the emotion words people actually used in essays and blog posts. A larger *negative* emotion vocabulary went with slightly more distress and worse health, while a larger positive one went with better well-being. The link was small, and it may simply reflect that people in pain spend more time in those words. Still, the lesson is accuracy. A precise word for one feeling beats a thesaurus of gloom.

## When does naming a feeling stop helping?

Labeling has edges, and most popular articles skip them.

It can get in the way of reframing. [Nook, Satpute and Ochsner (2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42761-021-00036-y) asked people to name their emotion and then reappraise or accept it. Naming first made both strategies work less well. In their studies, naming alone also didn't reduce negative feeling, which differs from the UCLA results and suggests the details of how and when you name matter. Their guess: putting a label on a feeling can make your first read of the situation more solid, so it's harder to see it another way. The samples were small and each regulation window lasted only seconds, so take it as a caution, not a verdict.

It can slide into rumination. One label is information. The same label rehearsed for forty minutes at 2 a.m. ("I'm so anxious, why am I like this, I'm always anxious") is a loop. Labeling works by giving the feeling a name and then letting your attention move on. If naming turns into analyzing why you feel it, and then into why you're the kind of person who feels it, you've crossed from labeling into brooding. The piece on [rumination vs reflection](/blog/rumination-vs-reflection) covers how to tell the two apart.

It turns down good feelings too. Remember the fourth study in the 2011 paper. If you're watching your kid's school play, you probably don't need to file a mood report.

And it isn't treatment. If you're naming the same heavy feeling day after day, a label is a clue to bring to a doctor or therapist, not a fix. If you're in crisis, use the numbers on our [crisis resources](/crisis-resources) page now.

## Does mood tracking help, or make you feel worse?

Mood tracking is affect labeling done on a schedule. The evidence for it is promising, mixed, and thinner than app stores suggest.

The best-known trial is [Kauer and colleagues (2012)](https://doi.org/10.2196/jmir.1858). Young people aged 14 to 24 with mild or worse depressive symptoms used a phone to record either their mood, stress and activities, or only their activities, for two to four weeks. The mood group gained more emotional self-awareness, and that gain went along with lower depressive symptoms at six weeks. In a community study of an app called MoodPrism, [Bakker and Rickard (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jad.2017.11.016) found that people who engaged more with daily mood check-ins saw drops in depression and anxiety over 30 days. Gains in emotional self-awareness explained the effect, but only for people who started out clinically depressed or anxious. That study had no control group.

The caution comes from a [2018 systematic review by Dubad and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0033291717001659). Across 25 studies of mood-monitoring apps in young people, the evidence was "promising but limited," with few high-quality trials. The authors also flagged that adult studies suggest mood apps could increase negativity and depressive symptoms for some users.

Our read: the benefit runs through awareness. The active ingredient seems to be noticing something you didn't know, and a logged number alone doesn't guarantee that. Tracking that only produces a chart of how bad you felt can feed the same loop it's meant to break. That's why, in Auserene, the mood on a note is something the evening conversation can ask you about, rather than a score to watch.

## How do you name a feeling well, in ten seconds?

Here's a short method that follows the research. It's meant for the middle of a day, not for a long journaling session.

1. Place it on two axes. Is this pleasant or unpleasant, and how strongly? Is your energy high or low? Many psychologists describe feelings with these two dimensions, valence and arousal ([Posner, Russell and Peterson, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1017/s0954579405050340)). "Unpleasant, high energy" already rules out sadness and points toward anger, fear or agitation.
2. Pick one specific word. Not "stressed." Try overwhelmed, cornered, behind, resentful, uneasy, flat, homesick, embarrassed. If two fit, write both.
3. Name the cause in a few words. "Work, the 3 p.m. review." "Sleep, up at four." "Mom's call." This is the part that becomes useful later.
4. Stop. Don't explain it. Don't decide what it says about you. Put the phone down, refill the kettle, go back to what you were doing.

The stop is the hard part. Think of labeling as a glance at the feeling. Staring at it is a different activity, with different results.

For bigger feelings, the end of the day is a better place to go deeper, with questions that move you forward rather than in circles. Our list of [evening reflection questions](/blog/evening-reflection-questions) is a good start, and [journaling for anxiety](/blog/journaling-for-anxiety) covers what to write when worry is the main event.

## What do your labels add up to over a month?

A single label calms things a little. Thirty days of labels, each with a cause attached, can show you something you couldn't see from inside any one day.

Maybe "flat" clusters on days you skipped lunch. Maybe Sunday evenings are "uneasy" more often than not, and the cause you keep writing is "work tomorrow." Maybe "irritated" almost always comes with "sleep." None of this is diagnosis. It's the kind of pattern a good friend might point out if they'd been reading over your shoulder.

This is also where the research suggests caution. The [Dejonckheere and colleagues (2019)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-019-0555-0) analysis of 15 studies and 1,777 people found that complex measures of how moods move added little to the simple question of how good or bad people felt on average. The useful patterns in a mood log are usually plain ones: this feeling, this cause, this often. You don't need sophisticated charts. You need someone, or something, to notice the repeat.

## Is writing feelings down better than thinking them?

The studies above used several formats. People chose words on a screen in the 2007 brain-imaging study, described their feelings during exposure in the spider study, and posted them publicly in the Twitter analysis. We haven't found good evidence that one format beats another.

Writing does have a practical edge: it leaves a record. That record is what turns a single label into a pattern. It's also what expressive writing research builds on, though those effects are modest. A [2006 meta-analysis of 146 studies by Joanne Frattaroli](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823) found an average effect size of r = .075 for writing about personal experiences. That's real, and small. If you want the fuller picture of what journaling does and doesn't do, start with [does journaling actually help](/blog/does-journaling-work).

A quick note with one specific word and one cause sits between the two. It's short enough to do in the moment, which is when labeling works, and it's written down, which is what makes a month of them worth reading.

## The word, and then the rest of the day

Naming a feeling won't solve the thing that caused it. The deadline is still there. The text you're waiting for still hasn't come. What changes is small and physical: the alarm in your body drops a notch, and you get a few seconds to decide what to do instead of reacting.

Do it with a precise word. Add the cause. Then go back to your day. A month from now, the words you wrote while it was happening may tell you more about your life than any summary you'd write in hindsight.
