To journal for anxiety, write each worry down in one line when it shows up, with a guess at how likely it is, and then come back to it later that day to check what actually happened. The writing gets the worry out of your head. The coming back is what teaches you the worry was wrong. In clinical trials with people who have generalized anxiety, the version of journaling that tracked outcomes reduced worry more than simply recording thoughts, and roughly nine in ten feared outcomes never happened.

Most advice stops at the first half. Get it out, dump it on the page, free your mind. That helps a little, and the research below says how little. The bigger gain sits in a step almost nobody tells you to take.

## Does journaling actually help anxiety?

Yes, modestly, and the honest size of the effect matters if you're deciding whether to bother.

The best-studied form is expressive writing, the method James Pennebaker introduced in the 1980s ([Pennebaker and Beall, 1986](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.95.3.274)). You write about a stressful experience for 15 to 20 minutes, three to five times ([Baikie and Wilhelm, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1192/apt.11.5.338)). A 2006 meta-analysis by Joanne Frattaroli pooled 146 randomized studies and found a real but small average effect, r = .075 ([Frattaroli, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823)). A 2023 meta-analysis by L. Guo, covering 31 trials and 4,012 people, found a small effect on depression, anxiety, and stress combined (Hedges' g = −0.12) that tended to show up at follow-up rather than right after writing. Sessions spaced one to three days apart did better than sessions spaced a week or more apart ([Guo, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1111/bjc.12408)).

Small still counts. Treat journaling as a tool rather than a treatment, and expect the way you do it to change how much you get.

There's also a short-term cost worth knowing. Expressive writing usually raises negative mood right after the session, according to the same review, though that dip did not appear to cause lasting harm. If you feel a bit worse after writing about something hard, that's common and expected. If you feel worse for days, change the method (more on that below).

Gentler forms of journaling have evidence too. In a 2018 trial led by Joshua Smyth, 70 medical patients with elevated anxiety wrote online for 15 minutes, three days a week, for 12 weeks, answering positive prompts like "What are you thankful for?" Compared with usual care, they reported less anxiety and depression after the first month ([Smyth et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.2196/11290)). For a wider look at what the research does and doesn't support, see [does journaling actually help](/blog/does-journaling-work).

## Why does writing a worry down make it smaller?

Anxiety takes up room. A worry you are trying to hold onto, so you won't forget to dread it, uses the same mental workspace you need to think about anything else.

Two studies make this concrete. In 2011, Gerardo Ramirez and Sian Beilock had students write about their worries immediately before a high-stakes exam. Their scores improved, and the students who were habitually anxious about tests gained the most ([Ramirez and Beilock, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199427)). In 2018, Hans Schroder, Tim Moran, and Jason Moser asked chronic worriers to do expressive writing and then measured a brain signal that runs high in anxious people when they make a mistake. The signal was smaller after expressive writing than after neutral writing. The authors' reading is that writing may "offload" worries from working memory ([Schroder, Moran and Moser, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/psyp.12990)). That's a lab measure of the brain, and it fits what many people notice: the worry is still there on the page, and you can think again.

Putting a name to the feeling may help as well. In a 2007 brain-imaging study, Matthew Lieberman's team found that labeling the emotion in a negative image dampened the amygdala's response compared with other ways of processing it ([Lieberman et al., 2007](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x)). The effect has limits. A 2026 replication found that labeling can make it harder to reframe a feeling right afterward ([Ariely et al., 2026](https://doi.org/10.1007/s42761-026-00362-z)). We go further into this in [naming your feelings](/blog/name-your-feelings).

## The step most advice leaves out: checking what happened

Worry makes predictions. The meeting will go badly. The test result will be bad news. She's angry because she hasn't replied. Each one feels like information, and each one disappears from memory once the day moves on, so you never find out how often it was wrong. Your next worry gets to borrow the full authority of the last one.

Psychologists Lucas LaFreniere and Michelle Newman built a journal to close that loop. In their Worry Outcome Journal, people with generalized anxiety disorder recorded worries when prompted by text four times a day, rated how likely they thought the bad outcome was, and later tracked what actually happened. A comparison group logged their everyday thoughts and rated their distress, without following up. After 10 days, the outcome-tracking group had a larger drop in worry ([LaFreniere and Newman, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/da.22507)).

When they went back through the entries, 91.4% of the worried-about outcomes had not come true. The most common result for any one person was 100%. People whose worries proved untrue more often improved more ([LaFreniere and Newman, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beth.2019.07.003)).

A 2026 trial by J. T. Schwob and Newman repeated the test with a smartphone app and 117 people with GAD, prompted four times a day plus once at the end of each day. Outcome monitoring again beat plain thought recording on worry. Of the feared outcomes, 89.14% did not happen. In the remaining cases, participants coped better than they had expected 84.35% of the time ([Schwob and Newman, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.janxdis.2026.103160)).

> Writing the worry down gets it out of your head; coming back to it later is what proves it wrong.

The control groups in both trials were journaling. They wrote their thoughts down several times a day. They just never went back. That's the gap between journaling as a pressure valve and journaling as a way to recalibrate.

## How to journal for anxiety: a two-part method

The method below borrows from those trials and from worry postponement, a technique Tom Borkovec's group tested in 1983. In their study, people who confined worrying to a set time and place reported less daily worry than controls ([Borkovec et al., 1983](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(83)90206-1)). A 2024 trial found it reduced worry in people with GAD ([Krzikalla et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.32872/cpe.12741)).

### During the day: catch the worry in one line

When a worry surfaces, write one line. Not a page. Something like this:

1. The prediction, in plain words. "Dev will push back on my proposal in front of everyone."
2. How likely it feels, from 0 to 100.
3. How upset you are, from 0 to 10, and one feeling word if one comes.

Then tell yourself you'll deal with it this evening, and go back to what you were doing. Writing it down makes that promise believable, because you're not relying on memory to keep it.

A 2025 trial from Daryl O'Connor's group, with 186 people over 14 days, found that worry postponement worked better with specific if-then plans for when and how to worry later, something like "if I notice a worry, then I'll write it down and return to it at 7 p.m." People who added plans reported about 15 minutes less worry than people using standard postponement ([McCarrick et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2025.2590072)). The same trial found no improvement in sleep, so don't expect this to fix nights on its own.

Speed matters more than polish here. A worry you can record in 20 seconds on your phone gets recorded. A worry that needs you to find a notebook and a quiet table often doesn't. In Auserene, this is a quick note on the Today screen, a line or half a sentence, with an optional mood on a seven-step pleasant-to-unpleasant scale and a cause like work or family.

### In the evening: check it against the day

Set ten minutes at a regular time, ideally not the last thing before sleep. Read each line from the day and ask:

1. Did it happen? Fully, partly, or not at all.
2. If it happened, how did I handle it compared with what I expected?
3. What was my likelihood guess, and what would I guess now?
4. Is there anything here I can actually act on tomorrow? If so, write the first step.
5. What's left that I can't do anything about tonight?

That's the whole practice. Over a few weeks the record builds up, and it's harder for the 2 p.m. dread to claim it's always right when you have a page of evidence that it usually isn't.

The hard part is doing the second half. By evening the worry has often faded, or a new one has replaced it, and reading old worries back feels like picking at a scab. This is where a question from outside helps. Auserene's founder learned to journal from a therapist, and the journal worked because someone helped him make sense of what he wrote. When the sessions ended, the guiding hand went with them.

## Journal prompts for anxious days

Use these when the one-line method feels too thin, or on a day when worry has taken over more than one corner.

1. What am I predicting will happen? Write it as a sentence a stranger could check.
2. What's the evidence for it, and what's the evidence against it?
3. If it does happen, what's the first thing I would do?
4. What would I say to a friend who wrote this?
5. What part of this is mine to act on, and what part isn't?
6. What's one thing that went okay today that I'd otherwise forget?
7. Write about the situation using your own name instead of "I." ("Priya is worried because...")

The last prompt comes from Ethan Kross's work on self-distancing. In a set of seven studies, people who thought about a stressful task using their own name or "you" instead of "I" felt less distress before giving a speech, performed better according to outside raters, and saw upcoming stressors as challenges rather than threats. The benefit held for people high in social anxiety ([Kross et al., 2014](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035173)). It feels odd for about two sentences, then oddly calming.

## Can journaling make anxiety worse?

It can, in a predictable way. Writing the same fear in more detail every night, without new information, rehearses it.

A 2013 study by David Sbarra and colleagues makes the point sharply. Recently separated adults were assigned to expressive writing or neutral writing. People who were actively searching for meaning in their separation did worse on emotional outcomes, up to nine months later, when assigned to expressive writing rather than the control task ([Sbarra et al., 2013](https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702612469801)). The writing gave an already turning mind more to turn over.

Signs your journal has become a loop:

- You're writing the same worry in the same words for the fifth night.
- You feel worse after writing, and it doesn't ease by the next day.
- Your entries list fears but never ask whether any came true.

If that's you, shrink the writing and add the questions. Switch from "why is this happening to me" to "what happened, and what's the next step." Write about yourself in the third person. Or put the pen down and do something physical for ten minutes. The difference between going over a thing and getting somewhere with it gets its own piece in [rumination vs. reflection](/blog/rumination-vs-reflection).

## Should you journal before bed if anxiety keeps you awake?

A short, specific list can help, but the evening review is better done earlier.

In a 2018 Baylor study, Michael Scullin had 57 young adults write for five minutes before sleeping in a lab. Those who wrote a to-do list for the coming days fell asleep faster than those who wrote about tasks they had already finished, and the more specific the list, the faster they fell asleep ([Scullin et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000374)). So if a worry has an action attached, write the action, park it, and close the notebook. Save the outcome-checking for a time when you aren't lying in the dark. We cover the night version in more depth in [racing thoughts at night](/blog/racing-thoughts-at-night).

## When is a journal not enough?

Journaling is a self-help tool with small-to-moderate effects. It isn't a treatment for an anxiety disorder, and an app isn't one either, Auserene included.

Talk to a doctor or a licensed therapist if worry is most of your day, if it has lasted for months, if it's keeping you from work, sleep, or people, or if you're having panic attacks. Anxiety disorders are common: about a third of U.S. adolescents and adults have one at some point in their lives, according to the [National Institute of Mental Health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/topics/anxiety-disorders). Worry outcome monitoring is itself a piece of cognitive behavioral therapy ([LaFreniere and Newman, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1002/da.22507)), and a therapist can fit techniques like it to you. If you're thinking about harming yourself or you're in crisis, please reach out now: [crisis resources](/crisis-resources).

For the worry-outcome figures and other numbers from this research in one place, see [journaling statistics](/blog/journaling-statistics).

## What to try this week

Pick one recurring time in the evening. During the day, write each worry as one line with a number next to it. At the evening time, go back and mark what happened. Do it for a week and then read the week back.

Most people find the column of "didn't happen" longer than they expected. Real problems still deserve your attention. The next 2 p.m. prediction, with that column behind it, can have a little less of your afternoon.
