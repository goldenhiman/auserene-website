The best AI journal app depends on what you want the AI to do. As of October 2026, Day One suits a long-term archive with AI as an optional extra, Mindsera suits people who want their writing analyzed, Rosebud suits guided, therapy-informed prompts (its free plan ended on September 30, 2026), Apple's Journal is the free, end-to-end encrypted choice with no chat at all, and ChatGPT works if you change its settings first. Auserene, which we make, is built for one thing: an evening conversation that reads your notes from the day and remembers what you said last week.

This is Auserene's own blog, and Auserene is one of the apps below. We've tried to be fair anyway. Every fact about another company comes from that company's own website, help center, privacy policy or App Store listing, checked in October 2026, and we link to each page so you can check us. Where a company doesn't say something, the table says "not stated" rather than guessing. We've left out star ratings, because we can't verify them.

## What makes a journal app an "AI" journal app?

What happens after you finish typing. A plain journal stores the page. An AI journal does something with it: comments on it (a follow-up prompt, a summary, a title), analyzes it over weeks (emotions, themes, charts), or talks back, turning the day into a conversation that becomes the entry.

Memory cuts across all of these. It decides whether the app knows on Thursday what you told it on Monday, and it is the part you most need to be able to read and correct.

## Which AI journal app is best for what?

### Rosebud

Rosebud is built around guided reflection. Its [homepage](https://www.rosebud.app/) lists voice journaling, weekly reports, guided journals (including one based on Internal Family Systems) and an app that "remembers" past entries. Its [evening reflection](https://help.rosebud.app/daily-journaling/evening-reflection) asks about a highlight, a tension and something you learned, based on the Rose, Bud, Thorn exercise. Rosebud's help center says [long-term memory](https://help.rosebud.app/ai-analysis/long-term-memory) is on for paid Bloom subscribers, and it's candid that the app does better recalling topics than specific dates.

The big change for anyone searching "Rosebud alternatives": Rosebud's help center says that [effective September 30, 2026, the free plan was retired](https://help.rosebud.app/account/changes-to-the-free-plan), because its new memory system costs more to run. Existing entries stay and can be exported. On privacy, Rosebud's [privacy policy](https://help.rosebud.app/about-us/privacy-policy) (updated September 8, 2026) names OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Amazon Web Services and Groq as AI providers, says it has BAAs and zero-data-retention agreements with them, and says data sent to them is anonymized. It also says authorized staff access journal content only when needed to fix a technical issue.

Best for people who want structured, therapy-informed prompts on phone and laptop.

### Day One

Day One is a journal first, with AI added on top. The [pricing page](https://dayoneapp.com/pricing/) lists a free Basic plan with unlimited text entries and end-to-end encryption, Silver at $49.99 a year and Gold at $74.99 a year, with AI features in Gold only. Gold's [Daily Chat](https://dayoneapp.com/guides/ai-features/daily-chat/) asks you questions about your day and turns the conversation into an entry, and it builds memories of your routines and relationships. Day One's guide notes that reviewing or deleting individual memories isn't available in the iPhone, iPad or Mac apps.

Day One is unusually straight about the trade-off every talking journal makes. Its [AI features guide](https://dayoneapp.com/guides/labs/ai-features/) says content from end-to-end encrypted journals "is temporarily not end-to-end encrypted while being processed with AI." Some processing runs on your device through Apple Intelligence, some on Automattic's infrastructure, and some through OpenAI, and entries aren't used for training unless you've given explicit permission.

Best for people who want a long-running archive with photos across many devices, with AI as an add-on they can leave switched off.

### Mindsera

Mindsera leans toward analysis. Its [homepage](https://www.mindsera.com/) describes "Minds" that comment on your writing, a Call Mode for spoken back-and-forth, emotion tracking over weeks and months, a Big Five personality assessment, an "Ask Your Journal" feature and 52-plus framework templates. The free Curious plan covers basic journaling, habit tracking and AI prompting; Genius costs $14.99 a month or $129 a year.

Its [privacy policy](https://www.mindsera.com/privacy-policy) (effective September 9, 2026) is one of the clearer ones. It says writing is encrypted at rest and in transit, that "Mindsera does not currently use end-to-end encryption," that journal content "is not used to train or improve AI models," and that it uses OpenAI, Anthropic and Google, with zero data retention on eligible Claude requests and retention elsewhere depending on the provider's terms.

Best for people who like frameworks, charts and a thinking partner on the web as well as the phone.

### Stoic

Stoic is a routine app with journaling at its center: morning and evening check-ins, guided journals, quotes, breathing and meditation. Its [homepage](https://www.getstoic.com/) calls it "Free and private" and adds a newer option to "Personalize your journals with AI." Its [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/stoic-journal-mental-health/id1312926037) describes AI reactions to check-ins and prompts that adapt to context, with AI token limits that renew weekly, and sells separate premium and AI tiers as in-app purchases without naming the periods.

Stoic's [privacy policy](https://www.getstoic.com/privacy-policy) says it doesn't store full journal texts on its servers, that AI features are optional and need a subscription and consent, and that its providers (OpenAI, Anthropic or Google Gemini) must not train on your data. It doesn't describe encryption.

Best for people who want a daily structure and a wide spread of Apple devices.

### Reflectly

Reflectly, from Kodeon, is a mood journal built around a short daily check-in. Its [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/reflectly-journal-ai-diary/id1241229134) describes "AI-guided prompts" and a mood tracker that charts correlations, with Reflectly Premium in-app purchases from $4.99 to $59.99. Its [website](https://reflectlyapp.com/) offers it on iOS and Android.

The privacy policy linked from its site is a company-wide Kodeon policy that doesn't mention Reflectly, journal entries or AI providers, so we've marked training and encryption as "not stated." We couldn't find those answers on Reflectly's own pages. If they matter to you, ask the company before you write anything sensitive.

Best for people who want a quick daily check-in more than a long reflection.

### Apple Journal

Apple's Journal is free on iPhone and iPad and syncs to the Mac. Its [App Store listing](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/journal/id6447391597) describes entries with photos, audio and your state of mind, and suggestions built from your photos, outings and workouts "using on-device intelligence." Apple's [Journaling Suggestions privacy page](https://www.apple.com/legal/privacy/data/en/journaling-suggestions/) says that with default two-factor authentication and a passcode, all Journal entries are end-to-end encrypted.

It doesn't talk back. There's no chat and no memory of what you wrote last week. For some people that's the whole appeal.

Best for people who want privacy first and don't need the page to answer.

### ChatGPT as a journal

Plenty of people journal in ChatGPT. OpenAI's help center says that for services for individuals, including ChatGPT, "we may use your content to train our models," and that you [opt out](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/5722486-how-your-data-is-used-to-improve-model-performance) under Settings, then Data controls. The same page says rating a reply with a thumbs up or down can make that whole conversation available for training even after you opt out. [Memory](https://help.openai.com/en/articles/8590148-memory-faq) can be reviewed and corrected through a memory summary, though OpenAI says the summary doesn't necessarily include every detail it can reference. The [pricing page](https://chatgpt.com/pricing) notes the Go plan may include ads.

Best for people who want one flexible tool and are willing to set it up. Our longer guide to [using ChatGPT as a journal](/blog/chatgpt-as-a-journal) covers the settings step by step.

### Auserene

Read this section knowing we wrote it. You leave quick notes during the day, a line or a mood, and in the evening it holds a short conversation about how the day went. It reads the day's notes first, asks about the parts that seem to matter, and remembers what you said last week. The conversation becomes that day's journal entry. Before bed, it can make a meditation written from that conversation. What it remembers lives in the You tab as plain sentences you can edit or delete.

Our [privacy policy](/privacy-policy) says entries are encrypted at rest with a key unique to you, that our language-model provider, Fireworks AI, works under zero-data-retention terms and is contractually barred from training on your words, that there are no ads or ad trackers, and that you can delete your account and everything in it from inside the app. It also says plainly that this is not end-to-end encryption: our servers and the AI provider process your text in memory to write a reply.

Best for people who want the evening conversation and a memory they can read.

## Does an AI journal actually help?

Journaling helps a little, on average, and how you do it matters more than which app holds it.

The baseline is modest. Joanne Frattaroli's [2006 meta-analysis of 146 randomized studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823) found that expressive writing works, with a small average effect (r = .075). Some formats do better in particular groups. In a [2018 randomized trial by Joshua Smyth and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.2196/11290), 70 medical patients with elevated anxiety either did 15-minute online positive-affect journaling sessions three days a week for 12 weeks or got usual care; the journaling group reported less distress and, after one month, fewer symptoms of depression and anxiety. It was a small, preliminary study, and it didn't involve AI.

Evidence on AI-assisted journaling is earlier still. In [MindfulDiary](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.05231), a 2024 CHI study by Taewan Kim, Young-Ho Kim and colleagues, 28 patients with major depressive disorder used a conversational journaling app built with psychiatrists for four weeks. Patients kept their daily records more consistently and in more detail, and their psychiatrists said the entries helped them understand their patients' days. It was a small usability study, so it says little about whether the app eased symptoms.

The risk is a journal that agrees with you. In 2025 OpenAI [rolled back a GPT-4o update](https://openai.com/index/expanding-on-sycophancy/) that had become "validating doubts, fueling anger, urging impulsive actions, or reinforcing negative emotions," and named mental health among its safety concerns. Agreement is a poor guide when you're writing through a hard week. Susan Nolen-Hoeksema's [2008 review](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x) found that rumination worsens depression, deepens negative thinking and impairs problem solving. A journal that replays the same worry with you is doing the opposite of reflection. (More on that difference in [rumination vs reflection](/blog/rumination-vs-reflection).)

How much you use it may matter too. In a four-week randomized study of 981 people by MIT Media Lab and OpenAI researchers ([Fang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473)), participants who chose to use a chatbot more had consistently worse outcomes on loneliness, emotional dependence and problematic use. The link is an association, and the study can't say the chatbot caused it. It still argues for a journal you close at night.

> A good AI journal asks the next question and then lets you go to bed.

So judge any app on practical things. It should ask more than it agrees, help you notice what changed since the last time you wrote about something, and have an end.

## How do you choose an AI journaling app?

Start with what you want the AI to do, then check the rest on the company's own pages, not in a roundup (this one included).

1. Decide on the kind of AI. Prompts and summaries suit Day One or Stoic, analysis suits Mindsera, and a conversation that becomes the entry is what Rosebud, Day One's Daily Chat, ChatGPT and Auserene each do in different ways.
2. Ask whether you can read the memory. An app that remembers you should show you what it remembers in words you recognize, and let you edit or delete it.
3. Find the training default. Is your writing used to train models, is that on or off by default, and are the app's AI providers barred from it by contract?
4. Know the encryption you're getting. End-to-end encryption and encryption at rest are different things. Any app that sends your words to an AI model has to process them unencrypted at that moment, and Day One says so in plain words. In the US, the Department of Health and Human Services says HIPAA usually [doesn't protect data you enter into apps for personal use](https://www.hhs.gov/hipaa/for-professionals/privacy/guidance/cell-phone-hipaa/index.html), so the privacy policy is what you have.
5. Check how durable the free tier is. Rosebud's ended this year. Read what a plan includes today and whether you can export your entries.
6. Match the platform to your life. If you journal on a laptop, an iPhone-only app won't fit.
7. Try a week of evenings, and notice whether you close the app calmer or more wound up. For help with what to write, our [evening reflection questions](/blog/evening-reflection-questions) work in any app or on paper.

## Questions people ask about AI journal apps

### Is there a free AI journal app?

As of October 2026, Mindsera's Curious plan includes AI prompting, ChatGPT has a free tier with limited memory, Apple Journal is free with on-device suggestions, and Auserene's free tier includes three Help me write nudges a day. Day One's AI features need Gold, and Rosebud retired its free plan on September 30, 2026.

### What is the best Rosebud alternative?

It depends on what you liked about Rosebud. For a conversation that turns into an entry, look at Day One's Daily Chat or Auserene. For analysis and frameworks, Mindsera. For a free option with no AI chat, Apple Journal.

### Are AI journal apps private?

Some are more private than others. An app whose AI runs on a server has to read your words to reply, so end-to-end encryption can't cover that moment. Read each app's privacy policy for three things: whether your writing trains models, which AI providers see it and under what terms, and how deletion works.

### Can an AI journal replace therapy?

No. An AI journal can help you notice patterns and put a day into words, but it doesn't diagnose or treat anything. If you're struggling, talk to a doctor or a mental health professional.
