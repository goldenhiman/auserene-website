The best evening reflection questions are concrete and short, and they come in an order: what happened, how it felt, what went well and why, what is still open, and what you will do first tomorrow. Questions about specific events help you process the day. Abstract ones like "why am I like this?" tend to keep you up. Five minutes is enough, and ending on a specific plan for tomorrow has better sleep evidence behind it than ending on a recap.

Most lists of end-of-day journal prompts treat every question as equal. They aren't. Some questions settle a day. Others reopen it at 11 p.m. and leave it running. What follows is a set of questions sorted by what the research says each kind does, an order to ask them in, and the few you should drop.

## What makes a good evening reflection question?

A good evening question points at something that happened. "What did I say in the 3 p.m. meeting, and what did I mean?" can be answered and then put down. "Why do I always mess these things up?" can't, so your mind keeps working on it after the notebook is closed.

Psychologists have a name for that difference. In a 2008 review in *Psychological Bulletin*, [Edward Watkins](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.134.2.163) looked at decades of work on repetitive thought, the habit of going over things again and again. He found it can be useful (recovering from upsetting events, planning ahead) or harmful (feeding depression and anxiety), and one of the main things that decides which is the level of the thinking. Abstract, general thinking about a bad experience tends to go badly. Concrete thinking about the details of what happened tends to go better.

That doesn't mean "why" is banned. In a 2005 study, [Ethan Kross, Ozlem Ayduk and Walter Mischel](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01600.x) asked people to recall an argument that made them angry and then think about it in different ways. Asking why they felt that way helped when they pictured the scene from a distance, as an observer would see it. It let them think about the event without the anger flaring back up. The trouble comes from asking why while you are back inside the scene, replaying the words. We cover that distinction in more depth in [rumination vs reflection](/blog/rumination-vs-reflection).

So the working rule for a good question is this. Ask about events, ask about specifics, and if you ask why, step back first.

## In what order should you ask them?

Order matters because each step makes the next one easier. Facts calm things down before feelings come in, and you name the feelings before you go looking for meaning. You close with a plan so the open loops have somewhere to go.

1. What actually happened today? Facts only, in the order they happened.
2. What did I feel, and when? One or two words per moment.
3. What went well, and why did it go well?
4. What is still unfinished or bothering me?
5. What is the first concrete thing I'll do about it tomorrow?

Each step has some evidence behind it, of varying strength.

Step two is naming. In a 2007 brain-imaging study, [Matthew Lieberman and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x) found that putting a word to a feeling ("angry," "scared") reduced activity in the amygdala, a region tied to emotional reactions, compared with other ways of processing the same images. One lab study isn't proof your Tuesday will feel lighter, but it fits what many people notice: "tense since lunch" is easier to hold than a vague knot. Our piece on [naming your feelings](/blog/name-your-feelings) goes further.

Step three is the classic "three good things" exercise. Steps four and five matter most for sleep, and we'll come back to them.

## Evening reflection questions to use tonight

These are grouped by the kind of day you had, because a hard day and a flat day need different questions. Pick two or three. Nobody needs all of them.

### For an ordinary day

1. What is one moment from today I'd remember if I had to?
2. Where did my energy go up, and where did it drop?
3. What did I do today that I'd been putting off?
4. Who did I talk to, and how did I feel afterward?
5. What did today ask of me that I didn't expect?
6. What would I tell a friend about today in two sentences?

### For a hard day

1. What exactly happened, in order, without the commentary?
2. What did I feel at the worst moment? Name it in one word.
3. If a friend told me this story, what would I notice that I'm missing?
4. Which part of this is mine to deal with, and which part isn't?
5. What helped, even a little?
6. What is one small thing I can do tomorrow about it?

Question three is the distance step from the Kross study, put into plain words. Picturing a friend telling the story is an easy way to step outside it.

### For a good day

1. What went well, and what did I do to make it go well?
2. Who was part of it?
3. What do I want to remember about how this felt?
4. What would I like more of next week?

### To close any day

1. What is still open in my head right now?
2. For each open thing, what is the next physical action, and when?
3. What can wait until tomorrow without anything breaking?
4. What do I want the first hour of tomorrow to look like?

> A good evening question can be answered, and then you can put it down.

## Which questions make things worse?

Some popular prompts are better left out of the evening, at least on a bad day.

"Why did this happen to me?" and "What's wrong with me?" are the clearest cases. They are abstract and they point at the self, which is the combination Watkins's review links to unhelpful repetitive thought. They also have no last line. You can answer them forever.

Replaying the scene is the other one. Writing out every word of an argument so you can relive it is the immersed version that Kross's study separated from the useful kind. If you notice yourself rehearsing what you should have said, switch to the facts-only question, or to "if a friend told me this story."

Long lists are a quieter problem. A thirty-question routine turns reflection into homework, and homework is the first thing to go when you're tired. Two good questions done most nights beat ten done for a week.

## Do gratitude questions actually work?

Yes, a little, and less than the internet says.

The best-known evening exercise is "three good things." In [Martin Seligman and colleagues' 2005 study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.60.5.410) in *American Psychologist*, people were randomly assigned to one of several online exercises or a placebo exercise. Writing down three things that went well each day for a week, and [explaining why each one happened](https://ggia.berkeley.edu/practice/three-good-things), was one of the exercises that raised happiness and lowered depressive symptoms over the follow-up period. The "why" in that exercise points at a good event, which is a different job from asking why about a bad one.

Then the replications came in. When [Myriam Mongrain and Tracy Anselmo-Matthews](https://doi.org/10.1002/jclp.21839) repeated the study in 2012 with a stronger control group, the exercises raised happiness, but so did a "positive placebo" that simply had people think about positive things about themselves. On depression, the exercises did no better than the control. The authors concluded the effects on depression may be more modest than first thought.

The pooled evidence says the same. A [2016 meta-analysis by Don Davis and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/cou0000107) found gratitude interventions beat a neutral activity on psychological well-being by a small margin (d = 0.17), did no better than other active psychological exercises, and showed no clear effect on anxiety. Earlier, [Robert Emmons and Michael McCullough](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.84.2.377) found that people who kept gratitude lists did better than comparison groups on several, though not all, well-being measures, with the most consistent gain in positive mood.

So keep one gratitude question in your evening if you like it. It's cheap and it tends to lift mood a little. Just don't expect it to carry anxiety on its own, and don't force it on a day that was bad. "What helped, even a little?" is a more honest version for those nights.

## Should you reflect right before bed?

It depends on which way the questions face.

In a 2018 sleep-lab study, [Michael Scullin and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1037/xge0000374) had 57 young adults write for five minutes before bed. Half wrote a to-do list for the next few days. Half wrote about tasks they had already finished. The to-do group fell asleep significantly faster, and the lab measured that with polysomnography rather than asking them. And the more specific the to-do list, the faster people fell asleep. For the completed-tasks group the trend went the other way.

That lines up with older work on unfinished goals. In 2011, [E. J. Masicampo and Roy Baumeister](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0024192) showed that unfinished tasks cause intrusive thoughts and distraction, and that making a specific plan for them was enough to stop those effects, even though the task itself was still undone. A plan tells your mind it can let go for now.

Two small studies point the same way. [Colleen Carney and William Waters](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15402010bsm0401_2) gave 33 students with insomnia a "constructive worry" routine: write down worries along with possible solutions. Over five nights, pre-sleep arousal dropped compared with a group that only wrote down worries. A [2011 pilot by Nancy Digdon and Amanda Koble](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1758-0854.2011.01049.x) with 41 students found that constructive worry, imagery distraction and a gratitude exercise each improved sleep compared with baseline, with no difference between them. That's a pilot without a no-treatment group, so treat it as a hint. Similarly, a [2009 survey by Alex Wood and colleagues](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jpsychores.2008.09.002) linked gratitude to better sleep through more positive and fewer negative thoughts at bedtime, but it was a single questionnaire and can't show cause.

The practical version is simple. Do the looking-back part of your reflection earlier in the evening, after dinner or on the commute home. Keep the last few minutes before bed for steps four and five: what's open, and the first concrete action for each. If you want the forward-facing step to stick, phrase it as an if-then plan ("When I sit down at 9, I'll reply to Sam first"). A [2006 meta-analysis by Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1) of 94 tests found plans like that had a medium-to-large effect on actually doing the thing (d = 0.65). If your mind still races once the light is off, our guide to [racing thoughts at night](/blog/racing-thoughts-at-night) has more.

If you've been sleeping badly for weeks, or the thoughts at night are heavy and won't let up, talk to a doctor or a therapist. A journal can sit alongside that help, but it can't replace it. If you're in crisis right now, [these resources](/crisis-resources) can connect you with someone today.

## Why a question from someone else lands differently

There's a limit to asking yourself questions. You already know the answers you're willing to give. When writing is studied on its own, the effects are real but small. [Joanne Frattaroli's 2006 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823) of 146 randomized studies of expressive writing found a positive average effect, r = 0.075. That's a small number. We look at what that means in [does journaling actually help](/blog/does-journaling-work).

What changes things is a good follow-up. In a 2012 experiment, [Frédéric Nils and Bernard Rimé](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.1880) had people watch an upsetting film and then talk about it with someone close to them. The listener was told to respond in one of several ways. Warm, empathic responses made people feel closer and say they felt better. But when they were tested again two days later, real emotional recovery showed up only when the listener had prompted them to think it through, by helping them look at the event a different way. Comfort felt good, and the follow-up questions did the processing.

That's the part a list of prompts can't do. A list asks "what went well?" and accepts whatever you write. A person asks "you said the meeting was fine, but you mentioned it three times. What happened in it?"

Auserene's founder, Himanshu Pathak, learned this the slow way. A therapist taught him to journal, and the journal worked because someone helped him make sense of it. When the sessions ended, that help went with them. That gap is why the evening conversation in Auserene exists.

## How long should an evening reflection take?

Five to ten minutes is enough. The Scullin sleep study used five. The three good things exercise takes about that long.

A routine that works for a lot of people:

1. During the day, jot a line when something happens. A word for the mood, half a sentence about why.
2. In the early evening, read those lines and answer two questions from the lists above.
3. Right before bed, write down what's still open and the first action for each, as specifically as you can.

The daytime notes are what make the evening part fast. At 9 p.m. most of the day has already blurred together. A line written at 2:40 ("snapped at Priya, tired, not about her") gives you something concrete to ask about.

## Close the day before you go to sleep

An evening review is there to finish the day. Ask what happened, name what you felt, notice one thing that went right, and give each loose end a time and a first step. Then stop. Tomorrow's version of you has a list and can start from there.
