Holiday stress mostly comes from five places: money, too little time, expectations that the days will feel a certain way, friction with family, and missing someone who isn't there. The research-backed moves are plain ones. Lower and say out loud what you expect, decide in advance what you'll do in the moments you can predict, name what you feel when a dinner turns tense, and write about a hard gathering afterward instead of replaying it at 2 a.m. Protect your sleep, go easy on the alcohol, and plan something for January.

Below is what the surveys say people find hard, what the studies behind each move found, and what you can say at the table when it starts to go wrong.

## What causes holiday stress?

Money comes up first, year after year. In a [2023 poll for the American Psychological Association](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2023/11/holiday-season-stress), The Harris Poll surveyed 2,061 U.S. adults online in mid-November. Nearly nine in ten (89%) said something about the season caused them stress, such as not having enough money, missing loved ones, or expecting family conflict. Spending or not having enough money was the most common answer (58%), followed by finding the right gifts (40%) and missing family or loved ones (38%). Forty-one percent said their stress goes up between November and January compared with the rest of the year, and 36% said the holidays feel like a competition.

The same poll is a reminder that "the holidays" doesn't mean the same thing to everyone. Among people of other non-Christian faiths, 57% said they didn't feel part of the U.S. holiday season, as did 45% of Jewish respondents and 29% of Christians.

Grief tops the list in a second poll series, from the American Psychiatric Association, a different group with the same initials. Morning Consult surveys about 2,200 adults for it each November. In [its November 2025 release](https://www.psychiatry.org/news-room/news-releases/americans-more-anxious-about-the-holidays), 41% expected more holiday stress than the year before, up from 28% in 2024. The top worries were missing someone or grief (48%), affording or finding gifts (46%), challenging family dynamics (32%) and loneliness (31%). In [the 2024 version](https://www.psychiatry.org/news-room/news-releases/one-quarter-of-americans-say-they-are-more-stresse), difficult family dynamics came in at 35%.

Politics has its own line. After the 2024 U.S. election, [an APA survey of 2,083 adults](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2024/12/avoid-politics-holiday) found 72% hoped to avoid discussing politics with family over the holidays and 38% said they were avoiding family they disagreed with.

These are U.S. opinion polls, and they can't show what causes what. They aren't all gloom either. In the 2023 poll, 84% said the season creates a sense of togetherness, and 72% agreed it can feel bittersweet.

So holiday stress comes as a handful of problems piled into five or six weeks. The rest of this guide takes them one at a time.

## Why do the holidays feel worse than you expected?

Part of it is the gap between the holiday in your head and the one you get.

Psychologists call our predictions about future feelings affective forecasts. In [a 2005 review](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.0963-7214.2005.00355.x), Timothy Wilson and Daniel Gilbert describe a common error they call the impact bias. People overestimate how strongly and for how long an event will affect them. One reason is focalism. You picture the dinner, and forget the delayed flight and the kid with a cold that will also shape the day.

What the day is about matters too. In a small study titled ["What makes for a merry Christmas?"](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1021516410457), Tim Kasser and Kennon Sheldon surveyed 117 adults aged 18 to 80 just after Christmas 2001. People whose holidays centered on family and religious or spiritual experiences reported more well-being. People whose holidays centered on spending and receiving gifts reported less. It was one small survey and can't show that cutting back on gifts would make anyone happier, but it fits the polls.

Before the season starts, write one sentence about what you want it to feel like ("quiet," "close to my brother," "over without a fight") and the two small things that would produce that feeling. Cross off one obligation that serves the picture in your head more than that sentence. Then tell the people you live with. A lot of holiday resentment starts with expectations nobody said out loud.

## How do you deal with family during the holidays?

Decide in advance. You already know who will ask about your weight, your job or your love life, and which uncle will bring up the election. That predictability helps you.

The tool is called an implementation intention, an "if X happens, then I will do Y" plan. In [a 2006 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1), Peter Gollwitzer and Paschal Sheeran pooled 94 independent tests with more than 8,000 participants and found if-then plans helped people reach their goals with an effect of d = 0.65. That's a medium-to-large effect.

One of the early experiments was set over Christmas. In [a 1997 study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.73.1.186), Gollwitzer and Veronika Brandstätter asked German university students, a week before Christmas, to write a report on how they spent Christmas Eve and mail it in within two days. Half also wrote down exactly when and where they would write it. Of the 36 students whose reports could be matched, 71% of the planners (12 of 17) wrote it in time, against 32% of the others (6 of 19). A small sample and a homework task, but it shows how easily a plain intention gets lost over the holidays.

If-then plans also work on feelings. In [a 2009 set of experiments](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0013460), Inge Schweiger Gallo and colleagues showed people disgusting or frightening pictures. In the first study, 56 female students at the University of Konstanz either set the goal "I will not get disgusted," added an if-then plan ("And if I see blood, then I will stay calm and relaxed!"), or got no instructions. Only the if-then group reported less disgust. A later study with people afraid of spiders found the same pattern for fear. These were lab studies with pictures, and a family dinner is messier. Still, a plan tied to a specific cue beat a goal like "stay calm."

How to write yours:

1. List the three moments you can predict. "When Mom comments on my plate." "When Dan brings up immigration." "When it's 9 p.m. and everyone's still drinking."
2. For each, write one sentence. "If Mom comments on my plate, then I'll say 'I'm happy with it' and ask about her garden."
3. Make the "then" part something you can actually do in two seconds. Change the subject, refill someone's glass, go check on the kids.
4. Read them on the drive over. That's the whole trick. The plan has to be in your head before the cue shows up.

## What can you say when a family dinner gets tense?

Have a few lines ready, because you won't think of them on the spot. These work because they're true, short and don't invite a reply.

- For a comment about your body, job or relationship: "I'm good, thanks. How's the new house coming along?"
- For politics: "I don't think we'll change each other's minds tonight. Can we take this one off the table till after dessert?"
- For an old grievance: "I hear you. I don't want to get into it today. Can we talk about it next week, just the two of us?"
- For a pile-on: "I'm going to step out for some air." Then actually go.

The APA's tips in [its 2024 post-election release](https://www.apa.org/news/press/releases/2024/12/avoid-politics-holiday) are similar. Listen to understand, accept you probably won't change their mind, and know when to end the conversation.

In the moment, two small mental moves have evidence behind them.

The first is naming the feeling to yourself. In [a 2007 brain-imaging study](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x), Matthew Lieberman and colleagues at UCLA scanned 30 people as they looked at faces showing emotion. Picking a word for the emotion ("angry," "scared") dampened the amygdala's response more than other ways of processing the same faces. The effect in the lab was short and modest. But a silent "this is embarrassment" or "I'm furious that he said that" gives you a beat before you answer. There's more in [our piece on affect labeling](/blog/name-your-feelings).

The second is changing how you see the situation. A [2012 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0027600) by Thomas Webb, Eleanor Miles and Paschal Sheeran pooled 306 lab comparisons of emotion-regulation strategies. Perspective taking had one of the larger effects (d = 0.45), and reappraising the situation also helped (d = 0.36). Trying to suppress the feeling itself did nothing (d = -0.04), and dwelling on it made things worse (d = -0.26). At the table, that can mean asking yourself what your aunt is worried about when she asks, again, whether you're seeing anyone.

## How do you set boundaries with family over the holidays?

A boundary works best as a decision about what you'll do. "You can't talk about politics" asks your family to change. "If politics comes up, I'll go do the dishes" only asks you to follow through. The second is an if-then plan, which is why it holds.

The easiest ones to keep are about time, topic or money.

1. Time. "We'll come for lunch and head out around four." Say it when you accept the invitation, not at 3:55, and have your own way home.
2. Topic. Pick the one or two subjects you won't discuss this year and tell one ally at the gathering, so someone can help steer.
3. Money. Set a gift budget and say it early. "We're doing one small gift per person this year." Money tops the polls, so this one may save the most stress.

Expect pushback the first year. Say it once, kindly, then repeat the same sentence instead of explaining it again.

## What helps after a hard family gathering?

Try writing about it a day later instead of replaying it. The research starts with [a 1986 experiment](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.95.3.274) by James Pennebaker and Sandra Beall. Forty-six healthy undergraduates wrote for four days in a row about either the most upsetting experiences of their lives or trivial topics. In the months after, the students who wrote about their upsetting experiences made fewer visits to the campus health center. They also felt worse right after writing, with more negative mood and higher blood pressure. That dip is normal.

Later research shrank the effect without making it disappear. Joanne Frattaroli's [2006 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.132.6.823) of 146 randomized studies found expressive writing helps, with a small average effect (r = .075). It's a modest tool, and it's free.

A version for after a holiday:

1. The next day, set a timer for 15 minutes.
2. Write what happened and what you felt at each point, in the most exact words you can, including the part you're embarrassed about.
3. Write what you think was going on for the other person. This is perspective taking, done on paper.
4. Finish with one if-then plan for next time.

Do this earlier in the evening, not in bed. If the holidays are keeping you up, [our guide to racing thoughts at night](/blog/racing-thoughts-at-night) covers what helps when the replay starts after lights out.

## How do you cope with grief during the holidays?

Grief was the top holiday worry in the American Psychiatric Association's 2025 poll, ahead of money. Holidays bring the empty chair into focus. The rituals all assume the person is there, and every one that goes ahead without them marks the gap.

There's no study that tells you how to handle an anniversary or a first Christmas, Hanukkah, Diwali or Lunar New Year without someone. What helps people tends to be practical and personal.

- Decide on purpose whether to keep a tradition, change it or skip it this year. Not choosing tends to mean doing it the old way and being ambushed by it.
- Make room for the person. Say their name at the table, cook their dish, light a candle, tell one story.
- Have a way out. Tell one person you might need to leave the room, and that it doesn't mean anything is wrong.

Be kind to yourself about how you're coping, too. Self-compassion means treating yourself the way you'd treat a friend in the same week. In [a 2019 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1007/s12671-019-01134-6) of 27 randomized trials of self-compassion programs, Marissa Ferrari and colleagues found moderate improvements in stress (g = 0.67), depression (g = 0.66) and anxiety (g = 0.57) compared with controls, and a large one for rumination (g = 1.37). The authors flag possible publication bias and very mixed programs. In [five earlier studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.5.887), Mark Leary and colleagues at Duke found that self-compassion softened people's reactions to everyday negative events, and that inducing it helped people own their part in a bad event without being swamped by it. Those were short-term effects, mostly in questionnaires and lab tasks.

In practice that might mean saying to yourself, "This is a hard year. Of course I'm not enjoying the party. Lots of people grieving feel exactly this."

## What if you're spending the holidays alone?

Thirty-one percent of adults in the 2025 psychiatry poll said loneliness was a holiday worry. Some people are alone by circumstance, some by choice, and some because family isn't safe to be around. Being alone on a holiday is not a failure. It helps to plan the day rather than let it happen to you.

1. Plan the day the night before. One thing in the morning, one in the afternoon, one in the evening. A walk, a film, a call, a meal you actually cook.
2. Make one if-then plan for the hard hour. "If I start scrolling everyone's family photos, then I'll put the phone in another room and go outside."
3. Look for a shared table. Community centers, places of worship and volunteer groups often run holiday meals. In the 2023 APA poll, 16% of stressed adults said they volunteer to help others as a way to cope.
4. If the season isn't yours, say so to yourself. It's fine to treat December 25 as an ordinary day off.

## Do gratitude and savoring help with holiday stress?

They help a little. A [2016 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1037/cou0000107) by Don Davis and colleagues looked at gratitude interventions like writing down things you're thankful for. Compared with an activity-matched comparison, gratitude exercises improved psychological well-being by a small margin (d = 0.14). They didn't reduce anxiety (d = 0.11, not significant), and they did no better than other psychologically active exercises. Saying what you're thankful for at Thanksgiving is a nice ritual. Don't expect it to fix a stressful week.

Savoring means staying with a good moment on purpose: noticing it, telling someone about it, or remembering it later. In a [2012 daily diary study](https://doi.org/10.1080/17439760.2012.671345), Paul Jose, Bee Lim and Fred Bryant followed 101 people for 30 days. On days with good events, people who savored them more reported a happier mood. It was a correlational study, so it can't show savoring caused the better mood.

A fair approach is to pick one good moment from each gathering, like your niece's laugh or the walk after lunch, and spend 30 seconds on it. It won't cancel a hard day, but you'll remember more than the hard part.

## Do sleep and alcohol make holiday stress worse?

Sleep loss makes emotional reactions bigger. In [a 2007 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.08.007), Seung-Schik Yoo, Matthew Walker and colleagues split 26 healthy adults aged 18 to 30 into two groups. One stayed awake for about 35 hours, the other slept normally, and then both were scanned as they viewed upsetting images. Walker reported that the brain's emotional centers were over 60% more reactive in the sleep-deprived group, and the amygdala was less connected to the prefrontal region that helps regulate it. A short-fused you at the table may just be tired.

Alcohol helps you fall asleep and then breaks up the rest of the night. A [2013 review](https://doi.org/10.1111/acer.12006) by Irshaad Ebrahim and colleagues of studies in healthy volunteers found that at all doses, alcohol shortens the time to fall asleep and deepens the first half of the night, then disrupts the second half. REM sleep is delayed, and at moderate and high doses it is reduced.

Decide your drink limit before you arrive, as another if-then plan ("If I've had two, then I switch to sparkling water"). Keep your wake-up time close to normal. And leave the big conversation for the morning, not 11 p.m. after wine.

## Why do you feel low after the holidays?

The days after the holidays can be harder than the days themselves. A [2011 literature review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22247812/) by Randy and Lori Sansone looked at studies of mental health around Christmas. Mood may dip around the holiday, but psychiatric emergency visits, self-harm and suicide attempts tend to be lower around Christmas, then rebound afterward. The studies span different countries and decades, so treat this as a tendency. It points to the weeks after as the time to watch.

To soften the January dip, put something to look forward to in the second week of January. Check in on people who had a hard season, especially anyone grieving or alone. A message in mid-January can mean more than one on December 25. If you kept notes through the holidays, read them back to see what actually made the season hard.

## How good is the evidence, and when should you get help?

The polls are snapshots of what U.S. adults say worries them. Many of the experiments here were small lab studies with university students looking at pictures, a long way from a family dinner. The meta-analyses are larger, and they mostly show small to moderate effects. None of this will make a hard family easy. It gives you more options.

Holiday stress is ordinary. Some things are not. Talk to a doctor or a mental health professional if, for more than two weeks, you've felt low or numb most of the day, lost interest in things you used to care about, or struggled to sleep, eat or get through the day. Get help, too, if grief feels as raw months later as it did at first, or if being with family means being unsafe.

If you're thinking about hurting yourself or feel you can't go on, call or text 988 in the U.S. to reach the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/), or see [our crisis resources page](/crisis-resources) for other countries.

Auserene is a journal with an AI that asks questions. It isn't therapy and doesn't replace one.

## A season you can plan for

Most holiday stress is predictable, so it can be planned for. Say what you hope the season will feel like. Write three if-then plans before the first gathering, and keep a few lines ready for the table. Name the feeling when it hits, and write about the bad moments a day later instead of carrying them into January. Sleep, drink less than you're offered, and put something good in the calendar for mid-January.
