The best journal prompts for overthinking are concrete questions that have a last line. Ask what exactly happened, what you predict and how likely it is, what "good enough" would look like, or what you'd say to a friend who did the same thing. Match the prompt to the kind of overthinking you're doing (replaying, worrying, deciding, wondering what people think, self-criticism or the 2 a.m. loop), and write until nothing new turns up.

Below are 30 prompts sorted that way, each grounded in a named technique and each with a follow-up question, because your first answer is usually the one you've already rehearsed.

## What makes a journal prompt good for overthinking?

"Overthinking" isn't a clinical term. Psychologists study it under two names: rumination, which mostly faces the past, and worry, which mostly faces the future. A [2008 review by Susan Nolen-Hoeksema, Blair Wisco and Sonja Lyubomirsky](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x) concluded that rumination worsens low mood, makes negative thinking more negative, impairs problem-solving and gets in the way of acting.

A prompt for overthinking should have a last line. "Write about what's bothering you" fails that test, because you can answer it all night.

The clearest evidence for what works comes from Edward Watkins at the University of Exeter. In a [2012 randomized trial](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3359637/) with 121 people with major depression, a self-help program called concreteness training had people take a recent upsetting event and practice three moves: picture the sensory details, work out how it unfolded step by step, and name the specific next steps. Adding it to usual care improved depression scores, and the benefit held at six months.

A relaxation program did about as well on overall symptoms, but concreteness training reduced rumination more. That's why most prompts below ask for details, sequence and a next step.

The longer argument about "why" versus "how" questions is in [rumination vs reflection](/blog/rumination-vs-reflection). This piece is the practical companion.

## Which kind of overthinking are you doing?

Different loops need different questions. Find the one that sounds like your head right now.

1. Replaying. You keep rewinding the meeting, the comment, the text you sent.
2. What-if worry. You keep running a future that hasn't happened yet.
3. The decision loop. You could recite the pros and cons, and you're no closer.
4. "What did they think of me?" You left the conversation an hour ago and you're still in it.
5. Beating yourself up. The thought has moved past what happened to a verdict on who you are.
6. The 2 a.m. loop. The same sentence keeps coming back with nothing new attached.

If you can't tell, write one line about what's in your head and look at the verb. "Said," "did" and "happened" point to replaying. "Will," "might" and "what if" point to worry. "Should I" means a decision. If the page stays blank, Auserene's notes have a "Help me write" nudge, three a day on the free plan, for exactly that moment.

## Prompts for replaying something that already happened

These follow the three steps from Watkins's concreteness training: the details, the sequence, the next step. The aim is to swap the highlight reel your mind plays for the actual footage.

1. What happened, in order, as if you were writing stage directions? *Then: at which line did it start to go wrong?*
2. What could you see and hear at that moment? *Then: what's one detail you had forgotten until now?*
3. What did you do, and what did you leave undone? *Then: which of those was in your control, with what you knew then?*
4. Looking back, were there warning signs before it happened? *Then: would you honestly have spotted them in the moment?*
5. What is one specific thing you'd do differently next time? *Then: when will that chance come up, and what's the first step?*

Prompt two can feel beside the point. Sensory detail pulls you out of the abstract summary ("I humiliated myself") and back into a room with a temperature and a clock on the wall, where events have a size.

## Prompts for what-if worries about the future

Worry makes predictions and then never checks them. In a [2020 study by Lucas LaFreniere and Michelle Newman](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7233480/), 29 people with generalized anxiety disorder logged their worries for 10 days and then tracked whether each one came true over the following month. Of their worry predictions, 91.4% did not come true. People whose records showed more untrue worries improved more over treatment.

This was a small study of people with a diagnosed anxiety disorder, and worry is sometimes right. Writing a worry down as a prediction makes it something you can check, and checking teaches your brain what its forecasts are worth. Our piece on [journaling for anxiety](/blog/journaling-for-anxiety) covers the full method.

1. What exactly do I predict will happen, and by when? *Then: how likely is it, from 0 to 100?*
2. What would be the first sign that it's starting to happen? *Then: have I seen that sign yet, or am I reacting to the idea of it?*
3. If it did happen, what would I do in the first hour? *Then: who would I call, and what would I say?*
4. What happened the last few times I worried about something like this? *Then: what does that record suggest about this one?*
5. Is there anything I can do about this today? *Then: if not, what date will I check back on this prediction?*

When the date from prompt five comes, read the prediction and write one line about what actually happened. That line does more than reassurance can.

## Prompts for a decision you keep circling

Some people overthink decisions because they're trying to find the best option, when a good one would do. Barry Schwartz and colleagues called them maximizers. In their [2002 study](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.83.5.1178), people who scored high on maximizing reported less happiness, optimism and life satisfaction, and more regret and depression, than people who settle for "good enough." The data are correlational, so they can't show that maximizing causes the unhappiness.

The economist Steven Levitt ran a [field experiment](https://doi.org/10.1093/restud/rdaa016) in which people stuck on a decision, such as quitting a job or ending a relationship, flipped a coin on a website. Those the coin told to make a change were more likely to make it, and on average they reported being happier six months later. The participants chose to take part and were already undecided, so treat it as a hint rather than a rule. For a choice you've weighed for months, more thinking may not be what's missing.

1. What is the decision, in one sentence that can be answered yes or no? *Then: what's the real deadline, and who set it?*
2. What would "good enough" look like? Name three must-haves. *Then: which options meet all three?*
3. What new information would actually change my mind? *Then: can I get it this week? If not, I already have what I'm going to get.*
4. Imagine you flipped a coin and it landed on one option. What did you feel in the first second? *Then: what is that feeling telling you that the lists aren't?*
5. What is staying undecided costing me each week? *Then: what's the smallest step that would make one option more real?*

Prompt four borrows the coin without making it the judge. The flash of relief or disappointment is often your most honest data.

## Prompts for "what did they think of me?"

Faith Brozovich and Richard Heimberg, in a [2008 review](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2008.01.002), define post-event processing as going over your own performance after a social situation, again and again, and possibly reconstructing it as you go. It's closely tied to social anxiety.

In the [spotlight effect studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.78.2.211), Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec and Kenneth Savitsky found that people wearing an embarrassing T-shirt overestimated how many people in the room would remember it. In group discussions, people overestimated how much their own slips stood out. We start from our own vivid experience, the authors argue, and don't adjust enough for everyone else's.

In five studies on what they call the liking gap, [Erica Boothby and colleagues (2018)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797618783714) found that after a conversation, people consistently underestimated how much the other person liked them and enjoyed their company. It held for strangers in a lab and for new college roommates, where it lasted for months.

1. What did I actually say, as close to word for word as I can manage? *Then: which parts of the replay am I adding now?*
2. What did the other person do right afterward? *Then: what's the most ordinary explanation for it?*
3. If I'd been watching from across the room, what would I have noticed? *Then: would that observer still be thinking about it now?*
4. What signs were there that it went fine, such as a laugh, a follow-up question or a warm goodbye? *Then: why am I counting those for less than the awkward moment?*
5. Does anything actually need doing, such as a message or an apology? *Then: if nothing does, what will I do when the replay starts again?*

## Prompts for beating yourself up over a mistake

Self-criticism feels like taking responsibility, but it may not help you improve. In [four experiments](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167212445599), Juliana Breines and Serena Chen (2012) found that people prompted to treat a failure with self-compassion were more motivated to improve than people given a self-esteem boost or a neutral control. After failing a hard test, they spent more time studying afterward. After recalling something they'd done wrong, they reported more motivation to make amends and not repeat it.

In five studies, [Mark Leary and colleagues (2007)](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.92.5.887) found that when they induced a self-compassionate frame of mind, people acknowledged their own part in a bad event without being swamped by negative feelings about it. These are short-term effects, mostly in lab settings. They still undercut the idea that you must be harsh with yourself to do better.

Kristin Neff, who developed the concept, describes [three elements](https://self-compassion.org/what-is-self-compassion/): kindness instead of self-judgment, the recognition that everyone fails instead of the sense you're the only one, and a balanced awareness of the pain that neither hides nor inflates it. The prompts follow them, then turn to repair.

1. What did I do, told the way a fair witness would tell it? *Then: which part is mine to own, and which isn't?*
2. What would I say to a friend who did exactly this? *Then: write it again, to yourself, using your own name.*
3. Who else has made a mistake like this one? *Then: what did I think of them afterward?*
4. What am I feeling, in one or two words, without the verdict attached? *Then: where do I feel it in my body?*
5. What would repair or improvement look like, concretely? *Then: when will I do the first part?*

## Prompts for the 2 a.m. loop that won't stop

Some loops have no new content, just the same sentence, "I'm going to mess this up" or "they're angry with me," coming round every few minutes. Analyzing it gives it more airtime, so these prompts change how you hold the sentence instead of arguing with it.

Acceptance and commitment therapy calls this cognitive defusion. In a [small 2004 study](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2003.10.008), Akihiko Masuda, Steven Hayes and colleagues tested a technique first described about 90 years earlier: rapidly repeating a single word. With eight undergraduates and their own negative thoughts about themselves, it reduced both how believable and how upsetting those thoughts felt, more than distraction or trying to control the thought did. Eight people is a tiny sample, so treat it as a reason to try.

There's also some evidence for an old ritual: writing the thought down and throwing the page away. In [two experiments by Yuta Kanaya and Nobuyuki Kawai (2024)](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-024-57916-z), about 100 people received insulting written feedback, then wrote down what had upset them. Those who threw the page in the trash or shredded it saw their anger fall back to where it started. Those who kept it stayed angrier. The study measured anger, not worry, and only tested paper. In earlier work by [Pablo Briñol and colleagues (2013)](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797612449176), people who physically threw away written thoughts relied on them less afterward, more so than people who only imagined doing it.

1. What's the sentence that keeps coming back? Write it once, exactly as it sounds. *Then: how many times has it come round tonight?*
2. Circle the one word that carries the weight, such as "failure." Say it quickly, over and over, until it sounds like noise. *Then: write the sentence again, starting with "I'm having the thought that..."*
3. Is there anything new in it since the last time it came round? *Then: if not, write "nothing new" and leave it there.*
4. Does it need anything from me tomorrow? *Then: write that one task on a separate scrap and keep it.*
5. Tear off the rest of the page and throw it away. *Then: turn off the light.*

Keep this to about five minutes. For what to do once the light is off, see [racing thoughts at night](/blog/racing-thoughts-at-night), and for a calmer review of the day earlier in the evening, our [evening reflection questions](/blog/evening-reflection-questions).

## Why the follow-up question matters more than the prompt

Every prompt above comes with a second question on purpose. Your first answer to "what happened?" is usually the version you've already told yourself fifty times. The follow-up asks for something the rehearsed version left out.

In a [2012 experiment by Frédéric Nils and Bernard Rimé](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.1880), people watched an upsetting film and then talked it over with someone close to them, who'd been told how to respond. Warm, empathic listening made people feel closer and feel better in the moment. But two days later, real emotional recovery showed up only when the listener had prompted them to think the event through and see it differently. Comfort helped them feel better. The questions did the processing.

A printed list can't hear your answer. A friend would hear "it was fine, I guess" and ask what "I guess" was covering.

Himanshu Pathak, who built Auserene, ran into this gap himself. A therapist taught him to journal, and the journal worked because someone helped him make sense of what he wrote. When the sessions ended, the person asking the second question was gone.

## When do journal prompts make overthinking worse?

Prompts can feed the loop as easily as end it. Signs that a session has turned into rumination with a pen:

1. Nothing new has appeared on the page for ten minutes, and you're rewording the same point.
2. Your answers are drifting from "what happened" to "what this says about me."
3. You feel worse at the end than at the start, several nights running, with no new understanding to show for it.

If that happens, write down the one next step you can find and close the notebook.

Sometimes the problem is bigger than any prompt. The [National Institute of Mental Health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/generalized-anxiety-disorder-gad) describes generalized anxiety disorder as worry that's hard to control on most days for at least six months, with symptoms such as restlessness, poor sleep or trouble concentrating. If that sounds like you, or if overthinking comes with low mood that won't lift, talk to a doctor or a licensed therapist. A journal can sit alongside that help but can't replace it. ## Pick one group tonight

You don't need all 30 prompts, only the five that match your loop, follow-ups included.

Tonight, notice which kind of overthinking it is. Pick one prompt from that group, write until the follow-up gives you something you didn't already know, and then put the pen down. Whatever's still unsolved can wait on its own line until morning.
