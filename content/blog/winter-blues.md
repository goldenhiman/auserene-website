The winter blues are a common dip in mood and energy when the days get short, and most people feel some version of them. Seasonal affective disorder (SAD) is the far end of the same range: depression that comes back at the same time each year and gets in the way of work, sleep and relationships. For both, the best-tested help is morning light, either outdoors or from a 10,000-lux light box, plus keeping your days full of things you'd normally enjoy. Vitamin D, the most popular fix, has the weakest evidence.

That's the short version. The longer one matters, because how much effort makes sense depends on where you sit in that range, and the research is more uneven than most winter advice admits.

## What are the winter blues, exactly?

Most people notice the seasons in their mood. In [a 1989 telephone survey](https://doi.org/10.1001/archpsyc.1989.01810090065010) of randomly dialed households in Montgomery County, Maryland, Siegfried Kasper and colleagues at the US National Institute of Mental Health found that 92% of people noticed some seasonal change in mood or behavior. For 27%, those changes were a problem. Between 4.3% and 10%, depending on how strictly the researchers drew the line, described winters as hard as those of people diagnosed with SAD.

The authors concluded that SAD sits at the extreme end of a spectrum of seasonality that affects a large share of the population. "Winter blues" is the everyday middle of that spectrum: you sleep more, want carbohydrates, feel flat by 4 p.m. and would rather stay in. It's unpleasant. It usually doesn't stop you from functioning.

The survey covered one county, and people rated their memory of past winters, which is a shaky measure. It still set the frame most researchers use.

## How is SAD different from the winter blues?

SAD was first described in [a 1984 paper](https://doi.org/10.1001/archpsyc.1984.01790120076010) by Norman Rosenthal and colleagues at NIMH. They wrote up 29 patients whose depressions returned every year, mostly with oversleeping, overeating and craving carbohydrates. Most of that early group had bipolar disorder, especially bipolar II. That matters later, when we get to light boxes. A preliminary test in 11 of them suggested that extending the day with bright artificial light lifted their mood.

Today, doctors diagnose SAD as major depression with a seasonal pattern. According to the [National Institute of Mental Health](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/seasonal-affective-disorder), that means depressive episodes that start in a particular season for at least two years in a row, and more often in that season than at other times. The winter pattern often brings oversleeping, overeating, weight gain and pulling away from people.

Here's a rough way to tell them apart:

1. Winter blues: you're more tired and less cheerful, but you still get through the week, still enjoy things when you do them, and the mood lifts on a sunny weekend or a good evening with friends.
2. SAD: the low mood lasts most of the day, nearly every day, for weeks. You lose interest in things you'd normally like. Work, sleep or relationships start to suffer. And it has happened before, at the same time of year.

Plenty of people sit somewhere in between. The [NHS page on SAD](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/seasonal-affective-disorder-sad/) lists the same signs, including feeling hopeless, irritability and poor concentration, and also thoughts of self-harm, which always deserve a call to a doctor.

## Is seasonal depression even real?

Mostly yes, though how common it is remains debated.

In [a 1990 mailed survey](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-1781(90)90116-m), L. N. Rosen and colleagues sent a seasonal mood questionnaire to random households in four places: Nashua, New Hampshire; New York City; Montgomery County, Maryland; and Sarasota, Florida. Winter SAD and milder "subsyndromal" winter symptoms were more common the further north people lived. The pattern held mainly for people over 35. Like the Kasper survey, this relied on self-report and on people who chose to mail the form back.

A [2019 Cochrane review](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd011269.pub3) puts the prevalence of SAD at 1.5% to 9%, depending on latitude.

Then in 2016, Traffanstedt, Mehta and LoBello at Auburn University [looked for a seasonal pattern](https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702615615867) in a 2006 US government phone survey of 34,294 adults. Depression scores, measured with a standard eight-item questionnaire, were unrelated to latitude, season or sunlight. The authors argued the seasonal label should be dropped from the diagnostic manual. Critics answered that a condition affecting a few percent of people can be hard to see in a single snapshot survey that asks about the past two weeks, and that the study didn't follow anyone across seasons.

What to take from this: for most people, winter doesn't cause depression. For a smaller group the pattern is real, it repeats, and treatments aimed at it work.

## Does light therapy work for seasonal depression?

Yes, for SAD, with smaller effects than the early studies suggested.

In [a 2005 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.162.4.656), Robert Golden and colleagues searched nearly 30 years of light therapy research. Only 13% of the studies they found were rigorous enough to include. From eight trials, bright light reduced SAD symptoms with a large effect size of 0.84, and dawn simulation, a light that slowly brightens before you wake, also helped across five trials. The authors stressed how many published reports weren't based on solid designs.

A [2020 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1159/000502891) by Edda Pjrek and colleagues at the Medical University of Vienna used stricter rules. They took 19 randomized trials comparing bright light with dim light or a sham device, and pooled depression scores from 18 of them, covering 610 patients. Bright light still won, but the effect was smaller (a standardized difference of 0.37). People on bright light were about 1.4 times as likely to respond. The reviewers rated the trials as varied, mostly small or medium, and at moderate-to-high risk of bias.

So light therapy has been tested for over 40 years, it helps, and large, high-quality trials are still scarce. Part of the reason is practical: it's hard to fake a light box convincingly.

These trials were in people with diagnosed SAD. For the milder winter blues, there's very little direct research on light boxes.

## How should you use a light box?

Timing and dose matter more than the brand.

The [NIMH](https://www.nimh.nih.gov/health/publications/seasonal-affective-disorder) describes the usual approach as a box giving 10,000 lux, used for about 30 to 45 minutes a day, usually first thing in the morning, from fall through spring. The box should filter out UV light. The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/seasonal-affective-disorder-sad/) gives the same 10,000-lux minimum and also says it doesn't usually offer light therapy, because the evidence isn't strong enough for its standards. Both of those can be true at once.

Why morning? In [a 2001 study](https://doi.org/10.1001/archpsyc.58.1.69), Jiuan Su Terman, Michael Terman and colleagues at the New York State Psychiatric Institute measured melatonin in 42 people with SAD before and after 10 to 14 days of 10,000-lux light for 30 minutes. Morning light shifted their body clocks earlier, and the people whose clocks shifted most improved most. In a larger group of 80, early morning light did better than late morning or evening light. The best time, by their estimate, was about two and a half hours after the middle of your sleep. For someone who sleeps from midnight to 8, that's around 6:30 a.m. Most people get close enough by using the box soon after waking.

A practical version:

1. Talk to your doctor first if you have an eye condition, take medicines that make you sensitive to light, or have ever had bipolar disorder or a period of unusually high, wired energy. NIMH notes that eye problems and light-sensitizing medicines may mean you need supervised treatment or a different approach.
2. Use a box that gives 10,000 lux at the distance the maker states, with UV filtered out.
3. Sit near it soon after waking, while you eat breakfast or read.
4. Start at 30 minutes. In the Rohan trial below, therapists added 15 minutes a week if people weren't improving.
5. Give it two weeks before judging it. If it helps, keep going through the winter. The NHS notes that many people need treatment every winter to keep symptoms from coming back.
6. Ease off if you get headaches, eye strain or feel jittery, wired or unable to sleep, and tell your doctor. In [a 2005 review of light therapy protocols](https://doi.org/10.1017/s1092852900019611), the Termans warn clinicians to watch for hypomania, a mild form of mania, especially in the first few days.

## Is CBT better than light therapy for SAD?

In the short run they're about equal. Over the following winters, CBT seems to last longer.

Kelly Rohan at the University of Vermont designed a version of cognitive behavioral therapy for SAD, called CBT-SAD. In [her first trial in 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-006x.75.3.489), 61 adults with seasonal depression were randomized to six weeks of CBT-SAD, light therapy, both together, or a minimal-contact control. All three treatments beat the control by about the same amount. The combination had the highest remission rate, 73% against 20% in the control. Sixty-one people split four ways is small.

Then came the larger test. In [a 2015 trial](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.14101293), Rohan's team randomized 177 adults with recurrent winter depression to six weeks of group CBT-SAD (88 people) or 10,000-lux morning light therapy (89 people). Both cut depression scores about equally. Remission rates were nearly identical: 47.6% for CBT and 47.2% for light on the clinician interview. More people dropped out of the CBT arm (13 of 88) than the light arm (1 of 89), and about half of those who left never attended a session.

The more interesting part came later. The team [followed the same 177 people](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.15060773) for two more winters. In the first winter after treatment, the groups didn't differ. By the second winter, 27.3% of the CBT group had a recurrence on the clinician interview, against 45.6% of the light group, and more of the CBT group were in remission. A likely reason, though the trial didn't test it directly: light helps only while you keep using it, while CBT teaches skills you carry into the next winter.

The limits: it was one research team, in Vermont, comparing two specific protocols, and people knew which treatment they got. It's still the strongest head-to-head evidence there is.

## What does CBT for SAD actually involve?

According to [the 2015 paper](https://doi.org/10.1176/appi.ajp.2015.14101293), CBT-SAD combines two parts. The first is behavioral activation: you pick enjoyable activities and schedule them, to push back against winter's pull toward hibernating. The second is cognitive restructuring, which includes questioning beliefs about winter itself, like "the dark makes everything pointless" or "there's nothing to do until March." The program ends with a personal plan for spotting early warning signs next autumn.

Behavioral activation has good support on its own. In [a 2007 meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2006.11.001), Pim Cuijpers and colleagues pooled 16 randomized studies with 780 people of activity scheduling for depression, a method where people monitor their mood and daily activities and deliberately add pleasant ones. The effect against control conditions was large (0.87), and roughly equal to full cognitive therapy. Most of those studies weren't about seasonal depression, and many were small.

You can borrow the idea without a therapist:

1. Each evening, write down what you did that day and rate your mood from 1 to 10. One line each is plenty.
2. After a week, look back. Which activities came with the better numbers? Often it's small, specific things: a walk with a friend, cooking a real meal, an hour on a hobby.
3. Schedule two or three of those for the coming week, with a day and time. "Saturday 10 a.m., walk the canal with Dan" works better than "get out more."
4. Do them whether or not you feel like it. Behavioral activation works on the idea that action comes first and mood follows.
5. Notice the winter-specific thoughts as they show up ("it's too dark to bother") and write one more balanced line next to each one.

## Can journaling help with seasonal depression?

We haven't found a trial of journaling for SAD. But two parts of what works depend on writing things down.

First, seeing the pattern. The diagnosis itself is about timing, depression that returns at the same season year after year, and memory is poor at timing. Asked in March, most people can't say whether the slump started in October or after Christmas. A few lines a day give you a record. If next autumn you see the same slide starting in the second week of November, you can start the light box, the walks and the plans before it deepens, and you can show your doctor something concrete.

Second, the monitoring half of behavioral activation. Logging what you did next to how you felt is how you find out which activities actually help you, rather than which ones are supposed to.

What journaling won't do is treat depression on its own. If you write every day and the entries get darker for weeks, that record is a reason to get help.

## Do outdoor light and morning walks help?

Probably, and they cost nothing.

The best-known study is small. In [a 1996 Swiss study](https://doi.org/10.1016/0165-0327(95)00081-x), Anna Wirz-Justice and colleagues in Basel gave people with SAD either a daily one-hour morning walk outdoors for a week or half an hour of dim artificial light, which acted mostly as a placebo. The walk improved every depression self-rating, and the dim light improved none. By the clinician scale, half of the walkers remitted, against a quarter of the dim-light group. The walk also shifted their body clocks earlier. The abstract doesn't give group sizes, it lasted one week, and walking adds exercise to light, so you can't tell which did the work.

Exercise has its own evidence for depression. In [a 2024 network meta-analysis](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-075847) in the BMJ, Michael Noetel and colleagues pooled 218 trials with 14,170 people with depression. Walking or jogging, yoga and strength training gave moderate reductions in symptoms, and the effects grew with intensity. Only one study met the strictest standard for low risk of bias, so confidence was rated low for walking and very low for the rest. None of these trials were specifically about seasonal depression.

Put together, a morning walk in daylight stacks three things in your favor: light at the time your body clock responds to it, movement, and often a reason to see someone.

## Does sleep timing matter in winter?

It does, because of the clock research above. Morning light moves your body clock earlier, and bright light late in the evening pushes it later. Winter tempts most of us into the opposite: sleeping in through dark mornings, then screens late at night.

The [NHS](https://www.nhs.uk/mental-health/conditions/seasonal-affective-disorder-sad/) advice for SAD includes keeping a regular sleep and wake time, getting outside in daylight every day, and avoiding screens right before bed. They haven't been tested as a standalone SAD treatment, so treat them as support for the main ones. A fixed wake time, weekends included, also gives your morning light a steady anchor.

## Does vitamin D help the winter blues?

The evidence here is weaker than its popularity.

Less sun means less vitamin D, and low blood levels have been linked to depression in observational studies. The trials have mostly been disappointing.

In [a 2014 Danish trial](https://doi.org/10.1186/1756-0500-7-528), Frandsen and colleagues gave 34 healthcare workers with seasonal symptoms either 70 micrograms of vitamin D a day or a placebo for three months over winter. Both groups improved as spring came, and there was no difference between them. Thirty-four people is very small.

The biggest test is [VITAL-DEP](https://doi.org/10.1001/jama.2020.10224), published in JAMA in 2020 by Olivia Okereke and colleagues at Harvard. It randomized 18,353 adults aged 50 and over to 2,000 IU of vitamin D3 a day or placebo for a median of 5.3 years. Rates of depression and changes in mood scores were the same in both groups. The limit is that most participants weren't depressed or deficient to start with, so it says more about prevention in older adults than about treating SAD.

NIMH describes the vitamin D results for SAD as mixed. If a blood test shows you're low, taking it is reasonable for its own sake. Just don't rely on it as your main plan for winter mood, and check with a doctor since it can interact with some medicines.

## Can you prevent seasonal depression before it starts?

Possibly, but the evidence is thin. The [2019 Cochrane review](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.cd011269.pub3) by Barbara Nussbaumer-Streit and colleagues looked for trials of light therapy started before symptoms, in people with a history of SAD. Out of 126 full papers they assessed, only one small trial with 46 people qualified. Fewer people in the light groups developed SAD, but the result was too imprecise to rule out no effect, and the evidence was rated very low quality.

The firmer evidence is the lasting effect of CBT-SAD in the Rohan follow-up. If you've had a bad winter before, a sensible plan is to decide in September what you'll do in October: when you'll walk, which evenings you'll keep for people, and at what point you'll call your doctor.

## When should you see a doctor about winter depression?

See a doctor or a mental health professional if:

1. Low mood or loss of interest has lasted most days for two weeks or more.
2. It's affecting work, sleep, eating or relationships.
3. It has happened at the same time of year before.
4. Self-help like daylight, exercise and plans with people isn't making a dent after a few weeks.
5. You're thinking about a light box and have an eye condition, take light-sensitizing medication, or have a history of bipolar disorder.

Treatment for SAD is well established. Light therapy, CBT and antidepressants all have evidence, and a clinician can help you pick, and can check whether something else is part of the picture.

If you're having thoughts of hurting yourself or that you can't go on, please contact emergency services or a crisis line now. In the US you can call or text 988. You can find other lines at [our crisis resources page](/crisis-resources).

## Not a replacement for care

Auserene is a journal you talk to. It isn't therapy, and it can't diagnose or treat seasonal depression. It can help you keep the daily record that makes a pattern visible. If winter feels heavier than the blues, see a doctor, and bring the notes.

## Winter, planned for

You can't change the short days, but you can change what fills them. Get light early, outdoors if you can, and from a 10,000-lux box if your winters are hard and your doctor agrees. Fill the calendar with specific things you know lift you, and do them even when the sofa argues. Keep a line a day so next year you can see it coming. Don't count on a vitamin to do the work.
