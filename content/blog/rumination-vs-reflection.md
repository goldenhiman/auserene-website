Rumination is passive, repetitive thinking about what went wrong and why, and it tends to leave you where it found you or a little lower. Reflection is deliberate thinking about the same material that moves toward understanding or a next step. You usually stop ruminating by changing the shape of the thinking: step back from the event before you ask "why," switch to "how" questions when you are still close to it, put it on paper instead of replaying it, and end with one small action or a set time to come back to it.

That is the short answer. The longer one is more interesting, because the research behind it contains an apparent contradiction about the word "why," and sorting it out gives you the most useful rule in this whole topic.

## What is the difference between rumination and reflection?

Picture the meeting that went badly. At 11 p.m. you are still in it. You hear the pause after you spoke, you see your manager's face, and you ask yourself, again, why you always do this. Forty minutes pass. Nothing new has come up. The feeling is heavier than when you started.

That is rumination: going over your negative feelings, their causes and their consequences, without getting anywhere. Experiments have shown that this kind of focus [makes bad feelings stronger and last longer](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01600.x), and the psychologist Susan Nolen-Hoeksema spent her career tracking what it does over time. In a [2000 study in the *Journal of Abnormal Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1037/0021-843X.109.3.504), people who habitually responded to low mood this way were more likely to develop new episodes of depression, and rumination also predicted anxiety symptoms.

Reflection can cover the exact same meeting. The difference is in the motion of the thinking. You turn the event over on purpose, you notice something you hadn't noticed, and you come out with a sentence you can use: "I froze because I hadn't prepared the numbers, so next time I'll bring them."

Researchers have tried to pin down this difference with questionnaires. In 1999, Trapnell and Campbell [argued that "self-focus" had been measured as one trait](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.76.2.284) when it was really two unrelated ones with different motives, which they called rumination and reflection. That split, they suggested, explained an old puzzle in which people who pay close attention to themselves seem to have both more self-knowledge and more distress.

Treynor, Gonzalez and Nolen-Hoeksema then [split the standard rumination questionnaire](https://doi.org/10.1023/A:1023910315561) into two parts in 2003. They called the passive, judgmental part "brooding" and the intentional, problem-focused part "reflection." As a [later Stanford paper by Anson Whitmer and Ian Gotlib](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10608-011-9361-3) summarizes it, brooding was linked to more depressive symptoms at the time and predicted increases a year later. Reflection was also linked to more symptoms at the time, but it predicted *lower* symptoms a year on.

That detail matters. Reflection does not always feel better in the moment. It can feel like pressing on a bruise. The payoff shows up later.

There is a caveat. Whitmer and Gotlib found the clean split between brooding and reflection held in people who had never been depressed or had recovered, but it blurred in people who were depressed at the time. One reading of that result: when your mood is very low, even well-meant reflection can slide into brooding. Keep that in mind for the last section.

## Why does rumination feel like problem-solving?

Rumination feels productive. It has the texture of work: concentration, effort, the sense that one more pass will crack it. From the inside, it is easy to believe you are figuring something out.

The evidence says otherwise. A [2008 review by Nolen-Hoeksema, Blair Wisco and Sonja Lyubomirsky](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1745-6924.2008.00088.x) pulled together two decades of studies and concluded that rumination makes low mood worse, makes negative thinking more negative, and impairs problem-solving. It also gets in the way of acting and erodes social support. The same review links rumination to anxiety, binge eating, binge drinking and self-harm.

So the loop is costly. Distraction is the obvious exit, and the same review found that positive distraction does lift mood in experiments, but people who use it more are not consistently less depressed. The more workable move is to keep thinking, differently.

## Is it bad to ask yourself "why"?

This is where the research seems to disagree with itself.

Edward Watkins, a psychologist at the University of Exeter, has spent years testing the difference between abstract and concrete thinking about your own problems. In his [2008 review in *Psychological Bulletin*](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2672052/), he describes a study in which people wrote about a failure either in an abstract, evaluative way ("Why did you feel this way?") or in a concrete, experiential way ("How did you feel moment by moment?"). Among people who stayed preoccupied with the failure, only the "why" writers were in a worse mood 12 hours later.

Other work points the same way. In a [2005 study of 40 depressed patients and 40 never-depressed controls](https://doi.org/10.1037/1528-3542.5.3.319), Watkins and Moulds found that concrete self-focus improved social problem-solving in the depressed group compared with abstract self-focus. And a [2008 set of three experiments](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2672048/) found that people trained out of the abstract, evaluative mindset reacted less strongly to a later failure than people trained into it.

Read on its own, that body of work says: don't ask why.

Now take the [2005 study by Ethan Kross, Ozlem Ayduk and Walter Mischel](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2005.01600.x), titled, pointedly, "When asking 'why' does not hurt." They had 155 students recall a time they felt overwhelming anger at someone. Some relived it as if it were happening to them again. Others were told to take a few steps back and watch the conflict unfold as if it were happening to a distant version of themselves. Then each group focused either on what they felt or on why they felt it.

Only one combination helped. People who asked "why" from the distanced position showed less anger, on both a self-report and a hidden word-completion measure, and less negative mood overall. Asking "why" while immersed did nothing for them. A second study found the distanced "why" group described the event in more abstract terms, and that this shift explained the calmer response.

So is abstract "why" thinking bad (Watkins) or good (Kross)?

Watkins addresses this in a footnote. The two research lines use "abstract" to mean different things. In his work, abstract means evaluative: what does this say about me, what does it mean for my life. In Kross's work, abstract means cool and seen from outside, the opposite of reliving the hot details. Watkins treats emotional distance as a separate dimension.

Put the two together and you get a rule you can use:

> When you are close to it, ask how. Ask why only once you have stepped back.

"Why did I freeze in that meeting?" asked from inside the memory, with your stomach still tight, quickly becomes "why am I like this," which is brooding. The same question asked about a person you are watching from across the room tends to produce an answer.

## How do you step back from your own thoughts?

Psychologists call this self-distancing, and the methods are small enough to do on a bad night.

1. Watch it from across the room. Kross's instruction was to take a few steps back and watch the event happen to "the distant you." In a [2010 follow-up](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2881638/), Ayduk and Kross found that people who did this on their own showed less emotional and cardiovascular reactivity, and that the benefit ran through how they told the story: less recounting of what happened, more reconstruing of what it meant. Recounting is the replay. Reconstruing is seeing it differently.
2. Use your name. Across seven studies with 585 people, [Kross and colleagues (2014)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0035173) found that thinking about a stressful situation using your own name or "you," instead of "I," increased distance. Those participants were less distressed about giving a speech, performed better according to outside raters, and did less of the anxious post-mortem afterward. A [2017 brain-imaging study led by Jason Moser](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-017-04047-3) suggests this works without much effort, which makes it practical when you are tired.
3. Ask what you'd tell a friend. People reason more wisely about other people's problems than about their own. Grossmann and Kross called this [Solomon's paradox](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535400): across three experiments with 693 people, participants were better at seeing the limits of their knowledge and other perspectives when the problem belonged to someone else. Telling them to self-distance erased the gap, in younger and older adults alike.
4. Look from ten years out. Bruehlman-Senecal and Ayduk found in [seven studies (2015)](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0038324) that picturing a recent stressor from the distant future reduced distress, mostly by drawing attention to the fact that the situation will not last.

None of this is avoidance. In the Kross study, people still faced the feeling. They just faced it from a few feet away.

## Does writing it down help, or make it worse?

Writing helps for negative events, with one twist for positive ones.

In a [2006 set of studies](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.90.4.692), Lyubomirsky, Lorie Sousa and Rene Dickerhoof asked students to process their worst experience for 15 minutes a day over three days, either by writing, by talking into a recorder, or by thinking privately. Four weeks later, the writers and talkers reported better life satisfaction and better mental and physical health than the thinkers. A likely reason is that thinking alone lets the event stay loose and looping, while writing forces it into sentences that have an order and an end.

Writing also seems to create distance on its own. In two longitudinal studies, [Park, Ayduk and Kross (2016)](https://doi.org/10.1037/emo0000121) found that people who did expressive writing about a distressing experience later reflected on it from a more distanced view, and that this predicted less emotional reactivity a month later (and six months later in the second study). Writers who gained the most distance used more causal words ("because," "so"), fewer negative emotion words and fewer "I"s as the days went on.

Here is the twist. In the same 2006 paper, students who wrote about their *happiest* day, especially those asked to analyze it, ended up with lower well-being and worse physical health than those who simply replayed it. Picking apart a good memory seems to drain it. So write to untangle the hard days, and let the good ones be.

The effects in these studies are real but modest, and many of the samples were university students. Writing helps at the margins. It won't undo a long-running pattern by itself. For the wider evidence on what journaling does and doesn't do, see [does journaling actually help](/blog/does-journaling-work).

## A ten-minute method for a ruminating evening

Use this when you notice you are on the same loop for the third time. If you keep quick notes during the day, in Auserene or a pocket notebook, start from what you wrote down at the time, which is usually less dramatic than the version in your head at 11 p.m. A good test is to ask yourself, "Has anything new come up in the last ten minutes?" If the answer is no, you are brooding, not reflecting.

1. Write the event in three or four sentences, in the third person. "Priya froze when Dan asked about the numbers. She went quiet and let him move on." Put your own name where the example says Priya. It feels odd for about a minute.
2. Separate what happened from what you made it mean. Under the paragraph, write one line starting "What she took it to mean:" and finish it honestly. ("That she isn't good at this job.")
3. Ask the "why" questions from the distance. Keep the third person.
   - Why might she have frozen at that moment, and not earlier?
   - What would she tell a friend who described this?
   - How will this look to her a year from now?
4. Switch to "how." Now ask concrete questions, in the spirit of the ones Watkins tested.
   - How exactly did it unfold, step by step?
   - How could she prepare differently next time?
   - What is the smallest next step, and when?
5. End with an action or an appointment. If there is a step, write it down with a time. If there isn't one tonight, schedule the thinking for tomorrow. In a [2025 randomized trial of 186 adults over 14 days](https://doi.org/10.1080/08870446.2025.2590072), McCarrick and colleagues found that postponing worry worked better when people made a specific if-then plan for when and how they would come back to it. That group reported about 15 minutes less worry a day than people using postponement alone. It did not improve sleep, which is worth knowing.
6. Close the page. You have written the event, the meaning, a wider view and a step. The loop has fewer reasons to restart.

If you already keep a journal, this fits in the last ten minutes of the evening. If you struggle to know what to ask yourself, start with our list of [evening reflection questions](/blog/evening-reflection-questions).

A question asked by someone else can shift the vantage point by itself. Himanshu Pathak, who built Auserene, learned to journal from a therapist, and he says the writing worked because someone helped him make sense of it. Distance is easier to take when someone else offers it. A friend can do this, a therapist can, and so can a set of written prompts. What matters is that the questions are aimed at the event from a few steps back.

## When is rumination a sign you need more than a journal?

Self-distancing and writing are good tools for an ordinary bad day. They are not enough on their own if rumination has become the background of most days, if it keeps you awake night after night, or if it comes with low mood that won't lift.

There are treatments built for this. A [2013 systematic review by Querstret and Cropley](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cpr.2013.08.004) of 19 studies found that mindfulness-based and cognitive behavioral approaches can reduce both rumination and worry, online or in person. Watkins developed a version of CBT that targets rumination directly. In a [small 2011 trial with 42 people](https://doi.org/10.1192/bjp.bp.110.090282) whose depression had not responded to medication, adding it to usual care improved symptoms and remission rates. The authors note the trial had no attention-control group, so it can't separate the method from the extra therapist time.

If your thoughts keep circling back to how hard things are, talk to a doctor or a licensed therapist. They can tell rumination apart from depression or an anxiety disorder in a way no article can. ## Where to start tonight

You don't need to stop thinking about the thing that hurt. You need to stand somewhere else while you think about it, and you need the thinking to end in a sentence you can act on.

Tonight, if the loop starts, try one change only. Write the event down using your own name. Then ask what you'd tell her, or him, or them. See whether the answer comes faster from over there.
