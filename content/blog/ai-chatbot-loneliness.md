Talking to an AI chatbot can make a lonely evening feel lighter, and for some people it seems to help for a while. The best 2026 research suggests it rarely fixes loneliness itself, though. Over months, heavier use for companionship tends to go along with more emotional isolation, especially for people who have few others to talk to. The pattern that seems safest is to use a chatbot to sort out what you feel and then take it to a person.

The studies don't all point the same way, and the differences between them say a lot about who gets helped and who doesn't.

## Can talking to an AI chatbot make you lonelier?

It can, though the effect in the research is small and the cause is hard to pin down. The clearest evidence comes from a study that followed people for a year.

In [a 2026 paper in *Psychological Science*](https://doi.org/10.1177/09567976261427747), Dunigan Folk and Elizabeth Dunn at the University of British Columbia surveyed 2,149 adults in the UK, US, Canada and Australia four times over 12 months. Each time, people reported how often they had used chatbots for social reasons in the past four months (regular chats, advice on life decisions, companionship) and how emotionally isolated they had felt. About a quarter to 30% reported some social chatbot use at each wave.

The loop ran in both directions. Feeling more isolated than usual predicted more chatbot use four months later. And more chatbot use predicted a further small rise in emotional isolation at the next wave. When the researchers used a broader measure of social connection, the picture changed: feeling less connected still predicted more chatbot use, but chatbot use did not predict a later drop in connection.

The limits matter here. Everything was self-reported, the design was observational, and the authors call their own analyses exploratory and urge caution. Still, it's the longest look so far at ordinary adults, and the direction is hard to ignore. Loneliness seems to send people toward chatbots, and the chatbots don't seem to send them back.

## Does an AI chatbot help with loneliness at all?

Sometimes, in the moment. This is where the research gets less tidy, and it's only fair to give the hopeful findings their space.

Julian De Freitas and colleagues at Harvard Business School ran five studies for [a 2025 paper in the *Journal of Consumer Research*](https://doi.org/10.1093/jcr/ucaf040). In a lab study, a short conversation with an AI companion reduced loneliness about as much as talking with another person, and more than watching YouTube. In a week-long study, the companion gave a consistent dip in loneliness after each use. The strongest explanation was whether the chatbot made people feel heard. People also underestimated how much better they would feel.

There are stories at the far end too. In [a 2024 survey in *npj Mental Health Research*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s44184-023-00047-6), Bethanie Maples and colleagues at Stanford asked 1,006 students who used Replika about their lives. They were lonelier than typical student samples, yet still reported fairly high social support, and 3% said Replika had halted their suicidal thoughts. That's a self-selected group of users answering about an app they already liked, so it can't tell us what would happen to anyone else. It does tell us that for some people, at some moments, a chatbot was the voice that showed up.

So the honest summary is that a chatbot can ease loneliness for the next hour. What the newer studies question is what happens over the next few months.

## What did the 2026 loneliness studies actually find?

Beyond the year-long survey above, two studies from this year and one from 2025 do most of the work. They measured different things, which is part of why the headlines about them sound so different.

### A two-week experiment: a stranger beat a supportive chatbot

The cleanest test is [a 2026 experiment in the *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology*](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jesp.2026.104911) by Ruo-Ning Li, Elizabeth Dunn and colleagues at UBC and the University of Pennsylvania. They randomly assigned 296 first-semester students to one of three things for two weeks. One group texted a randomly paired fellow student each day. One group texted "Sam," a chatbot built to listen, validate feelings and check in. The third group wrote one sentence about their day each evening, as a control.

Only the students texting a real person ended up less lonely. The chatbot group did no better than the one-sentence journal. The chatbot did lift mood in the short term compared with journaling, but that lift never added up to a sense of belonging.

The text logs offer a clue about why. The bot expressed more empathy than the human partners did. The students, though, gave much less empathy back to the bot than they gave to each other. Friendship seems to need the giving half, the turn where you support someone else, and a chatbot has no need for your support. About a third of the human pairs swapped contact details afterwards. Very few students kept talking to the bot.

The limits: these were first-year students with plenty of chances to meet people, most weren't severely lonely, and the human pairs met briefly in person first. Dunn has said AI companions might matter more for people who are truly isolated, such as some older adults. That hasn't been tested yet.

### Donated chats: who uses AI as a companion matters

The biggest look at real conversations is [a 2026 paper in *Nature Human Behaviour*](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41562-026-02516-2) by Yutong Zhang, Dora Zhao, Jeffrey Hancock, Robert Kraut and Diyi Yang, from Stanford and Carnegie Mellon. They surveyed 1,131 US adults who use Character.AI, and 237 of them donated their chat histories: 4,664 sessions and 464,687 messages.

People with smaller offline social networks were more likely to say companionship was their main reason for using the chatbot. Using it mainly for companionship went along with lower well-being. That link was stronger when people used it intensively, and when they disclosed a lot to it. In human relationships, opening up usually helps. With a chatbot, more disclosure tracked with feeling worse. According to [Stanford's write-up of the study](https://hai.stanford.edu/news/ai-companions-may-worsen-loneliness-for-vulnerable-users-stanford-study-finds), only about 12% named companionship as their main motive, yet more than half described the bot in their own words as a friend, companion or partner.

The limits: it's correlational, it covers one platform, well-being was self-reported, and the chats were coded with AI tools. It can't say whether the chatbot made anyone worse or whether people who were already struggling leaned on it harder. Probably both.

### The MIT and OpenAI trial: heavy users did worse

The 2025 study from MIT Media Lab and OpenAI researchers ([Fang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.17473)) randomly assigned 981 people to use ChatGPT daily for four weeks, by text or by one of two voices, with personal, impersonal or open-ended prompts. Which setup people got made no significant difference. How much they chose to use it did. Each extra minute of daily use went along with slightly more loneliness, less socializing with real people, more emotional dependence and more problematic use. People averaged about five minutes a day.

A companion paper ([Phang et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.03888)) analyzed more than 3 million ChatGPT conversations and surveyed over 4,000 users. It found that a small number of heavy users accounted for a disproportionate share of emotional conversations, and that very high use correlated with signs of dependence.

The limits are stated by the authors. There was no group that didn't use AI at all, the study ran over the 2024 election and holiday season, only 981 of 2,539 enrolled people finished, and OpenAI funded it, with four authors on staff. Daily use wasn't assigned, so heavy use could be a sign of a hard month as much as a cause of one.

## Who seems to be helped, and who isn't?

Put the studies side by side and a rough pattern shows up. It's a pattern, not a rule, and any one person can be the exception.

1. Short-term relief is real. Feeling heard lifts mood for a while, in the Harvard studies and in the UBC chatbot group alike.
2. Loneliness itself responds to people. In the UBC experiment, the one condition that reduced it involved another human, even a stranger.
3. People with fewer ties carry more risk. In the Character.AI data, a small offline network plus companionship use plus heavy use was the combination tied to lower well-being.
4. Heavy use is the warning light. Across the MIT and OpenAI work and the year-long survey, more use went with worse outcomes, whatever the setup.
5. Using it for something other than company looks different. In the Character.AI study, people who used it intensely but found it meaningful or felt good about how they used it didn't show the same drop.

The uncomfortable part is in point 3. The people most likely to turn to a chatbot for company are the ones with the least to lose and the least to fall back on.

## Why would a chatbot that listens well leave you lonelier?

Researchers are still working this out. Three explanations come up again and again, and each has some evidence behind it.

The first is that it's one-sided. The UBC logs show it plainly. The bot was more empathic than any 18-year-old texting partner, and it still didn't build belonging, because nobody was taking turns. Being needed is part of feeling connected.

The second is that it tends to agree with you. In [a 2026 paper in *Science*](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aec8352), Myra Cheng, Dan Jurafsky and colleagues at Stanford tested 11 leading AI models and found they affirmed users' actions 49% more often than people did, including when the user described deceiving or hurting someone. In three preregistered experiments with 2,405 participants, even one conversation with an agreeable model made people less willing to apologize or repair a conflict, and more sure they were right. People also rated the agreeable answers as better, trusted that model more and wanted to use it again. The authors note that the feature doing harm is the same one that keeps people coming back. For loneliness, that matters. If you're hurt that a friend hasn't called, a chatbot that agrees they've let you down makes it a little less likely you'll call them.

The third is substitution. Time and attention are limited. The MIT and OpenAI trial found that more daily use went with less socializing with real people. An hour with a chatbot is an hour you didn't spend texting your sister, even if the chatbot was kinder in the moment.

None of this means the people who built these tools wanted that outcome. It means a system that is always available, always warm and never needs anything back is a very easy thing to lean on, and an easy lean can turn into the only one.

## Is talking to ChatGPT when you're lonely bad for you?

Not in itself, and probably not if it's occasional and goes somewhere. A conversation at midnight, when no one else is awake, can help you get through the night. The research concern is a pattern: the chatbot becoming the main place you take your feelings, week after week, while the people in your life hear less from you.

People are already wary. When Pew Research Center surveyed 3,488 US adults in June 2026, [39% said chatbots do more to hurt than help](https://www.pewresearch.org/science/2026/08/25/do-americans-think-chatbots-help-or-hurt-people-using-them-for-loneliness-depression-or-stress/) people who use them for loneliness, and 19% said they help. Another 27% weren't sure. Adults under 30 were the most doubtful, with 49% saying hurt. That's opinion, not outcome data, but it tells you many users are uneasy about the same thing the studies are picking up.

If you've been using ChatGPT as a sort of diary, there's a separate set of questions about settings, memory and privacy. We cover those in [using ChatGPT as a journal](/blog/chatgpt-as-a-journal).

## Why do other people matter so much for loneliness?

Because the research on human connection is much older and much stronger than anything on chatbots, and it keeps finding the same thing.

In [a 2010 meta-analysis of 148 studies](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316) covering 308,849 people, Julianne Holt-Lunstad and colleagues found that people with stronger social relationships had about 50% higher odds of survival over the follow-up periods. That's observational data, but the effect held across age, sex and health status.

The ties that count aren't only close ones. In [a 2014 set of studies](https://doi.org/10.1177/0146167214529799), Gillian Sandstrom and Elizabeth Dunn found that students felt happier and more like they belonged on days they talked with more classmates than usual, and that brief chats with acquaintances counted. A barista, a neighbor, someone from your class.

And we tend to get these chats wrong in advance. Nicholas Epley and Juliana Schroeder [asked commuters in Chicago](https://doi.org/10.1037/a0037323) to talk to a stranger, sit in silence, or commute as usual. Talkers had the more pleasant ride. A separate group predicted the opposite, partly because they assumed strangers wouldn't want to talk.

This is where a chatbot could, in principle, help rather than replace. A [2011 meta-analysis of loneliness interventions](https://doi.org/10.1177/1088868310377394) by Christopher Masi, John Cacioppo and colleagues found that among randomized studies, the programs that worked best addressed the thinking that keeps lonely people stuck, such as expecting rejection or reading neutral signals as cold. A tool that helps you question "nobody wants to hear from me" and then sends you to test it is doing something useful. A tool that agrees with it is doing the opposite.

## How can you use an AI to reflect without replacing people?

If you're going to talk to an AI on a lonely evening, you can set it up so the conversation points outward. None of these steps has been tested as a package. Each one follows from the research above.

1. Name the feeling first. Ask it to help you put words to what you feel, as precisely as you can: left out, homesick, unseen at work, missing one particular person. In [a 2007 brain imaging study](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2007.01916.x), Matthew Lieberman and colleagues found that labeling feelings in words damped the amygdala's response to upsetting images. It was a small lab study using emotional images, not a study of loneliness, but naming is a cheap first step. (There's more on this in [name your feelings](/blog/name-your-feelings).)
2. Ask who the feeling is about. Loneliness is often specific. "I'm lonely" can turn into "I miss how things were with Priya before she moved." That points to a person.
3. Ask it to disagree with you. Tell it plainly: don't just agree, point out where I might be reading things wrong. Given the sycophancy findings, this one matters most. "She's clearly done with me" deserves a second opinion, and a chatbot left to its defaults won't give one.
4. Draft the message, then send it yourself. Use the chatbot to write the two-line text you've been putting off. Then close the app and send it. In [a 2023 paper](https://doi.org/10.1037/pspi0000402), Peggy Liu and colleagues ran experiments with more than 5,900 people and found that people consistently underestimate how much a friend will appreciate an unexpected check-in, more so when the friend isn't a close one.
5. Set an ending before you start. Ten or fifteen minutes is plenty. The trials that found harm tied it to amount of use, so the stopping point is part of the design.
6. Watch for substitution. Once a week, ask yourself two questions. Did I tell the chatbot something this week that I'd normally tell a person? Did I talk to fewer people than usual? If both answers are yes for a few weeks running, that's the pattern the research worries about.
7. Don't confide more than you would to a new acquaintance. The Character.AI study linked heavy disclosure to lower well-being. Save the deepest things for someone who can carry them with you.

A good evening session might go like this. You write that you feel invisible lately. The chatbot asks when you last felt seen. You realize it was the Sunday calls with your brother, which stopped in August. You draft "hey, miss our calls, free this weekend?" and send it before you lose your nerve. Total time, eight minutes. The chatbot did its job by making itself unnecessary.

## When should you talk to a person or a professional?

A chatbot is a poor fit for some kinds of loneliness, and the line is worth knowing.

Talk to a friend, family member or someone you trust if the loneliness has lasted weeks, if you notice you've stopped reaching out to anyone, or if the chatbot has become the main place you talk about your life. Even one honest conversation with a person tends to do something a chatbot can't.

Talk to a doctor, counselor or therapist if loneliness comes with low mood most days, trouble sleeping or eating, losing interest in things you used to enjoy, or drinking more to get through evenings. Loneliness and depression often travel together, and depression is treatable.

If loneliness comes with hopelessness or thoughts of harming yourself, please contact someone now. In the US you can call or text 988 to reach the [988 Suicide and Crisis Lifeline](https://988lifeline.org/). Elsewhere, our [crisis resources page](/crisis-resources) lists free helplines by country, and if you're in immediate danger, call your local emergency number.

## What the research can't tell you yet

Most studies are short. The UBC experiment ran two weeks, the MIT and OpenAI trial four. The only year-long study relied on people's own reports of how often they used chatbots and how isolated they felt.

Most can't separate cause from effect. The Character.AI and year-long studies show associations. Lonely people may use chatbots more, and chatbots may deepen loneliness, and both may be true at once.

The samples are narrow. First-year students, Prolific panels, users of one app, English-speaking countries. The groups that might gain the most, like isolated older adults, are the least studied.

The tools keep changing. A study of a 2024 model may not describe a 2026 one, and companion apps, general assistants and journaling tools are built for different jobs. Some of the companies doing this research also make the products.

So treat every finding in this piece, including the reassuring ones, as provisional.

## So, can AI help with loneliness?

It can help you get through a lonely evening, and it can help you work out what the loneliness is about. On the current evidence, it's much less good at the part that actually eases loneliness over time, which is other people, and heavy use for company tends to go with feeling more alone.

The practical answer is to use it as a rehearsal room, never as the destination. Name the feeling, find the person behind it, ask the chatbot to argue with you a little, write the message, and send it. Then put the phone down. If the chatbot is doing its job, you'll need it less as the weeks go by, and the people in your life will hear from you more.
